Research guide

Methods and research approaches

The manuscript develops completed arguments under their stated hypotheses. This chapter also explains approaches whose general applications remain unresolved. Its purpose is to make the calculation behind each approach intelligible before sending the reader to a technical note.

There are three different conclusions to distinguish. An identity rewrites the same finite quantity exactly. An estimate bounds its size, perhaps only from above. A volume theorem proves a limit for the original invariant and identifies the number in that limit with the volume of the specified knot complement. A useful identity or a sharp upper bound can be a completed result without being a volume theorem.

Throughout, KK is a fixed knot, N≥2N\ge2 is the quantum level, and KN(K)K_N(K) is the reduced Kashaev invariant; the unknot has value one. We use qN=eπi/N,ζN=qN2=e2πi/N. q_N=e^{\pi i/N},\qquad \zeta_N=q_N^2=e^{2\pi i/N}. Both roots occur in the literature. A formula written with qNq_N must not be compared with a formula written with ζN\zeta_N without translating that convention. All diagrams and twist numbers are fixed before NN tends to infinity. The notation chapter gives the conventions used in the detailed calculations.

The starting references are Kashaev’s triangulated construction, its R-matrix formulation, and the volume proposal. The Murakami–Murakami paper relates it to the colored Jones formulation. The finite invariant used here is specified directly; that relation is not a replacement for the source calculations.

The estimate and the geometric identification

Suppose an exact calculation splits the invariant into KN(K)=GN+RN. K_N(K)=G_N+R_N. Here GNG_N is a specified contribution, including its original scalar factors, and RNR_N is everything else. Neither term is automatically a topological invariant on its own.

A typical completed argument proves GN=aNeNS,log⁡|aN|N→0,|RN|≤eN(Re⁡S−η)+o(N) G_N=a_N e^{NS},\qquad \frac{\log|a_N|}{N}\longrightarrow0,\qquad |R_N|\le e^{N(\operatorname{Re}S-\eta)+o(N)} for a fixed η>0\eta>0, with aN≠0a_N\ne0 for sufficiently large NN. The complex number SS is the critical value of the action in that calculation. Dividing by GNG_N shows that RN/GN→0R_N/G_N\to0, and hence limN→∞2πNlog⁡|KN(K)|=2πRe⁡S. \lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{2\pi}{N}\log|K_N(K)| =2\pi\operatorname{Re}S. There is still a geometric assertion to prove: 2πRe⁡S=Vol⁡(S3\K). 2\pi\operatorname{Re}S =\operatorname{Vol}(S^3\setminus K). A formal critical point alone proves none of these three estimates. A positive comparison sum alone does not prove that aNa_N is nonzero. A representation of the knot group alone need not be the complete hyperbolic representation.

For the covered Conway constructions and decorated planar diagrams, the manuscript supplies all these steps. For an unchanged matched simple sphere with the stated Morse presentation and markings, the geometric contribution theorem supplies the contribution and its actual filled volume; the general signed remainder estimate remains open. This is why that partial theorem is included without being advertised as a full volume theorem.

Exact polynomials and shared face variables

A crossing circle is an auxiliary component bounding a disk pierced by two strands. Removing twists and inserting these circles gives an augmented diagram. After cutting its finite tensor calculation along the projection regions, assign one commuting variable LfL_f to each face ff. The same face has the same variable at every incident crossing region.

Put d=N−1d=N-1. For the complete-color source identities in this section take N≥3N\ge3. A local operator p(Ω)p(\Omega), where Ω\Omega is the specified pair Casimir matrix and pp is a polynomial, gives a homogeneous polynomial Hp(x,b,c,y)=∑i+k+ℓ+t=dap(i,k,ℓ,t)bixkyℓct. H_p(x,b,c,y) =\sum_{i+k+\ell+t=d}a_p(i,k,\ell,t)b^ix^ky^\ell c^t . The four variables label the four ordered faces adjoining that region. The coefficient apa_p is the corresponding finite matrix coefficient after the stated change to face coordinates. A box carrying a linear map is sometimes called a coupon in tensor-diagram notation; here it means precisely this local operator.

For the marked spherical construction with rr crossing regions, there are rr ordinary faces and two protected faces u,vu,v. Its shared polynomial is SN(𝝈)=[∏f≠u,vLfd]∏e=1rHe(𝑳;σe)|Lu=Lv=0. S_N(\boldsymbol\sigma) =\left[\prod_{f\ne u,v}L_f^d\right] \left.\prod_{e=1}^{r}H_e(\boldsymbol L;\sigma_e) \right|_{L_u=L_v=0}. The brackets mean coefficient extraction. The parameter σe\sigma_e is the spectral variable of crossing region ee. The two zero substitutions encode the original marked cut; they are not optional boundary conditions. The finite source proof and its general Morse version establish this identity with the scalar and endpoint conventions.

The terms central, left, and right describe supports of this local polynomial. With h=⌊d/2⌋h=\lfloor d/2\rfloor, central retains monomials for which both middle-face exponents i,ti,t are at most hh. The left piece has t>ht>h, overloading face cc; the right piece has i>hi>h, overloading face bb. Since i+t≤di+t\le d, both exponents cannot exceed hh. Expanding the product of these local choices splits SNS_N into its all-central part and its complement. These are sectors of one prescribed calculation, not new knot invariants. The geometric contribution theorem estimates the original filling functional applied to the all-central sector; a full-invariant theorem must also control the complementary sectors.

For a simple illustration of why the variables must remain shared, [z2](a+bz)(c+dz)=bd. [z^2](a+bz)(c+dz)=bd. Replacing the two factors by separate maxima over their zz-degrees would ignore the common target degree. In a knot diagram there are many such simultaneous constraints. The shared coefficient retains them before any asymptotic estimate.

Repeated spectral roots require derivatives as well as values. If J=cI+UJ=cI+U with U2=0U^2=0, then p(J)=p(c)I+p′(c)U. p(J)=p(c)I+p'(c)U. Thus knowing only p(c)p(c) does not determine the operator. This elementary example explains Hermite data: values and the derivatives prescribed by the multiplicities of the spectral roots. Derivatives in a spectral variable and derivatives in the generic quantum parameter are different data. Every use of a “normal term” in the notes must specify which derivative it means.

The finite filling estimate bounds the next operation, inserting a fixed signed crossing power nn. For the original linear functional ℒNn\mathcal L_N^n and a polynomial HH of degree at most N−1N-1, |ℒNnH|≤(1+|n|/2)supσ∈[−2,2]|H(σ)|. |\mathcal L_N^n H| \le (1+|n|/2)\sup_{\sigma\in[-2,2]}|H(\sigma)|. Its proof uses the exact values and derivatives at the spectral nodes. Applying it after the shared coefficient extraction adds no exponential cost at fixed twists. It does not estimate the supremum on its right.

There is a useful distinction between vanishing values and a vanishing functional. Write χN(z)=2TN(z/2)+2\chi_N(z)=2T_N(z/2)+2, where TN(cos⁡θ)=cos⁡(Nθ)T_N(\cos\theta)=\cos(N\theta). If HH vanishes at every root of χN\chi_N, including the odd-level simple root −2-2, the same theorem gives |ℒNnH|≤N−1N∥H∥[−2,2]. |\mathcal L_N^n H|\le\frac{N-1}{N}\|H\|_{[-2,2]}. The crossing-power factor disappears from this bound, but the derivatives of HH at double roots can still contribute. For the shared difference 𝒟N=SN,full−SN,central\mathcal D_N=S_{N,\mathrm{full}}-S_{N,\mathrm{central}}, the degree is at most N−1N-1 in each spectral variable and values vanish on the product of the spectral node sets. This is weaker than vanishing whenever any single variable is a node. Expanding the full functional in all variables cancels exactly its term with no derivatives and yields |(⨂e=1rℒNne)𝒟N|≤{∏e(1+|ne|/2)−∏e(1/N+|ne|/2)}∥𝒟N∥[−2,2]r. \left|\left(\bigotimes_{e=1}^{r}\mathcal L_N^{n_e}\right)\mathcal D_N\right| \le \left\{\prod_e(1+|n_e|/2) -\prod_e(1/N+|n_e|/2)\right\} \|\mathcal D_N\|_{[-2,2]^r}. The tensor product means that the functionals act successively on their respective spectral variables. This exact saving preserves the shared targets and both level parities. It leaves the size of the between-node polynomial as the remaining problem. The earlier Fourier–Hermite Gram estimate also has a level-independent filling cost, in a different positive Hilbert norm; the two estimates should not be identified.

Positive sums, factorial asymptotics, and angle optimization

The simplest variational argument applies to a positive sum. Suppose 𝒳N\mathcal X_N is a finite index set with |𝒳N|≤Nc|\mathcal X_N|\le N^c, and the weights wN(x)>0w_N(x)>0 satisfy a uniform logarithmic estimate log⁡wN(x)=NF(x/N)+o(N). \log w_N(x)=N F(x/N)+o(N). Here cc is fixed and FF is a continuous action on the limiting admissible set. The elementary inequalities maxxwN(x)≤∑xwN(x)≤|𝒳N|maxxwN(x) \max_x w_N(x)\le\sum_xw_N(x) \le|\mathcal X_N|\max_xw_N(x) give the exponential rate once admissible integer points approach a maximizer at every sufficiently large level. That last condition is an actual support question. A sequence of numerically favorable levels does not establish it.

Quantum factorials lead to integrals of logarithms of sine functions. The basic example is 1Nlog⁡∏j=1⌊Nt⌋2sin⁡πjN→∫0tlog⁡(2sin⁡πx)dx=−Λ(πt)π,0<t<1, \frac1N\log\prod_{j=1}^{\lfloor Nt\rfloor} 2\sin\frac{\pi j}{N} \longrightarrow \int_0^t\log(2\sin\pi x)\,dx =-\frac{\Lambda(\pi t)}{\pi}, \qquad 0<t<1, where Λ(θ)=−∫0θlog⁡|2sin⁡s|ds \Lambda(\theta)=-\int_0^\theta \log|2\sin s|\,ds is the Lobachevsky function. Near t=0,1t=0,1, the logarithmic singularities require endpoint estimates; uniformity there is not a consequence of a smooth Riemann-sum rule.

This positive-sum argument is a discrete Laplace principle. It is distinct from both majorization and a Tauberian theorem, even though all three exploit positivity or convexity. The positive Conway variational proof establishes the support and factorial estimates for its stated expressions. The geometric realization is a further argument. Positivity by itself neither constructs a knot complement nor proves invariance under changes of triangulation. A proposed positive tetrahedral model must also satisfy the appropriate local move identities with its full peripheral factors retained.

An angle structure on an ideal triangulation assigns positive dihedral angles to its tetrahedra. In each tetrahedron the three angles at a vertex sum to π\pi; around each manifold edge the incident angles sum to 2π2\pi. Opposite tetrahedron edges have equal angles. These are linear conditions, so the allowed assignments form a relatively open convex polytope, when it is nonempty.

For one positively oriented ideal tetrahedron with angle triple (α,β,γ)(\alpha,\beta,\gamma), its volume is V(α,β,γ)=Λ(α)+Λ(β)+Λ(γ),α+β+γ=π. V(\alpha,\beta,\gamma) =\Lambda(\alpha)+\Lambda(\beta)+\Lambda(\gamma), \qquad \alpha+\beta+\gamma=\pi. The total volume is strictly concave on the positive angle domain. This is the concavity used in the Casson–Rivin variational approach; the volume is not described as a convex function to be minimized. Stationarity in all allowed directions supplies the remaining length and gluing conditions. The peripheral equations and the actual face identifications still have to be retained. The marked positive gluing proof carries out these steps for its stated triangulation certificate.

For one tetrahedron this concavity can be checked directly. Use α,β\alpha,\beta as coordinates and put γ=π−α−β\gamma=\pi-\alpha-\beta. The negative Hessian of its volume is −∇2V=(cot⁡α+cot⁡γcot⁡γcot⁡γcot⁡β+cot⁡γ). -\nabla^2V= \begin{pmatrix} \cot\alpha+\cot\gamma&\cot\gamma\\ \cot\gamma&\cot\beta+\cot\gamma \end{pmatrix}. Its first diagonal entry is sin⁡β/(sin⁡αsin⁡γ)>0\sin\beta/(\sin\alpha\sin\gamma)>0, and its determinant is one, using cot⁡αcot⁡β+cot⁡βcot⁡γ+cot⁡γcot⁡α=1\cot\alpha\cot\beta+\cot\beta\cot\gamma+\cot\gamma\cot\alpha=1. It is therefore positive definite. This calculation also explains why one cannot assume that every individual cotangent is positive: one angle may exceed π/2\pi/2, while the constrained Hessian still has the required sign. Summing these quadratic forms and restricting to the allowed linear variations gives strict concavity for the entire positive angle structure.

One can see how the equations enter by differentiating: dV(𝜶̇)=−∑tetrahedron angle slots alog⁡(2sin⁡αa)α̇a. dV(\dot{\boldsymbol\alpha}) =-\sum_{\text{tetrahedron angle slots }a} \log(2\sin\alpha_a)\,\dot\alpha_a . An allowed variation 𝜶̇\dot{\boldsymbol\alpha} satisfies the homogeneous versions of the tetrahedron and edge equations. Requiring this derivative to vanish for every such variation is a coupled condition. Maximizing each tetrahedron separately would instead assign it three angles π/3\pi/3; those independent choices need not satisfy the edge equations of the given triangulation. This explains why adding local maximum volumes is not the variational problem used in the proof.

There are two logically different tasks here. One must find an interior critical point, and one must show that the cells and face pairings describe the intended manifold. Strict concavity proves uniqueness once those tasks have been met. It does not prove that every chosen triangulation has a positive solution. The geometry chapter explains the actual cells, cusp markings, and smooth fillings used in this project.

For the precise standard criterion on an orientable manifold with torus boundary, see Futer and Guéritaud, From angled triangulations to hyperbolic structures, Definition 3.1 and Theorem 1.2. Rivin’s 1994 paper gives the earlier variational setting. The marked gluing proof linked above establishes the version used by this source rather than inferring it from the appearance of a dilogarithm.

Weighted Cauchy and the cost of joining pieces

Let ℐN\mathcal I_N be the original finite index set, and write a scalar evaluation as ZN=∑α∈ℐNaαZ_N=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal I_N}a_\alpha. For any specified positive weights wαw_\alpha, |ZN|2≤(∑αwα)(∑α|aα|2wα). |Z_N|^2 \le \left(\sum_\alpha w_\alpha\right) \left(\sum_\alpha\frac{|a_\alpha|^2}{w_\alpha}\right). This is Cauchy–Schwarz applied to the vectors (wα)α(\sqrt{w_\alpha})_\alpha and (aα/wα)α(a_\alpha/\sqrt{w_\alpha})_\alpha. A useful choice of weights must make both factors estimable for the original contraction.

Weights can redistribute a cost, but cannot recover a phase discarded by the inequality. Indeed the infimum of the displayed right-hand side over positive weights is (∑α|aα|)2(\sum_\alpha|a_\alpha|)^2, attained by wα=|aα|w_\alpha=|a_\alpha| when no entry vanishes and approached otherwise. For a1=1,a2=−1a_1=1,a_2=-1, the exact sum is zero while even this optimized bound is four. The application therefore needs structural information about the joined source, not just an optimized positive weight for every separate term.

The matrix form says the same thing. If GG is a positive definite Hermitian matrix, vv is the source vector, and bb is the row implementing the original closure, then |bv|2≤(bG−1b*)(v*Gv). |bv|^2 \le (bG^{-1}b^*)(v^*Gv). The star denotes conjugate transpose. The first factor is the cost of the marked closure in the dual norm; the second is the source energy. Calling GG a convenient inner product does not bound either cost.

For a chain vj+1=Ajvjv_{j+1}=A_jv_j, a concrete sufficient estimate is Aj*Gj+1Aj≤e2εjGj,Gj>0. A_j^*G_{j+1}A_j\le e^{2\varepsilon_j}G_j,\qquad G_j>0 . The matrix inequality means that its right-hand side minus its left-hand side is positive semidefinite. Iteration and the closure estimate give |bvm|2≤(bGm−1b*)e2∑jεj(v0*G0v0). |b\,v_m|^2\le (bG_m^{-1}b^*)\,e^{2\sum_j\varepsilon_j}\,(v_0^*G_0v_0). Thus a useful varying norm must control the actual transport between successive norms and both endpoints. Making an intermediate matrix small by a change of basis can enlarge these endpoint factors. For a network, the analogous check must keep every shared boundary index and its measure.

When two pieces share a boundary label jj, the contraction still has one common sum over jj. If its original measure is a positive number θN(j)\theta_N(j), a useful exact form is Zjoined=∑jZA(j)ZB(j)θN(j). Z_{\mathrm{joined}} =\sum_j\frac{Z_A(j)Z_B(j)}{\theta_N(j)}. Set FA(j)=ZA(j)/θN(j)F_A(j)=Z_A(j)/\sqrt{\theta_N(j)}, and define FBF_B likewise. Cauchy bounds the square of the joined value by (∑j|FA(j)|2)(∑j|FB(j)|2)(\sum_j|F_A(j)|^2)(\sum_j|F_B(j)|^2). It gives no lower bound on their overlap: the two vectors can be orthogonal.

The whole-support boundary estimate and exact joining identity establish useful local inputs. For a reflected double, the two halves can be genuine complex conjugates in the specified pairing, giving a positive sum of squares. The reflection-double theorem proves precisely that identity. Its internal half-evaluations still contain their original phases. The general problem is to transport the estimates through the whole diagram and prove that the required contribution survives.

A second use of Cauchy’s name concerns coefficient estimates. For a polynomial P(𝑳)P(\boldsymbol L) with nonnegative coefficients, a target exponent vector 𝒕\boldsymbol t, and positive radii 𝝆\boldsymbol\rho, [𝑳𝒕]P≤P(𝝆)𝝆−𝒕,𝝆𝒕=∏fρftf. [\boldsymbol L^{\boldsymbol t}]P \le P(\boldsymbol\rho)\boldsymbol\rho^{-\boldsymbol t}, \qquad \boldsymbol\rho^{\boldsymbol t}=\prod_f\rho_f^{t_f}. The inequality follows by retaining one term of the positive evaluation. Minimizing the right-hand side is often called a capacity bound. There must still be one radius for each shared face, with all its incident factors evaluated there. For signed coefficients, the analogous elementary bound uses the polynomial obtained by taking absolute values of the coefficients, not |P(𝝆)||P(\boldsymbol\rho)|. For instance P(z)=1−zP(z)=1-z vanishes at z=1z=1 although its coefficient of zz has modulus one. The complete-source capacity obstruction shows that even optimizing all positive shared radii can overestimate the geometric rate in its stated source class. That obstruction concerns this comparison method, not the knot invariant itself.

For a worked positive example, take P(z)=(1+z)mP(z)=(1+z)^m and a target 0<k<m0<k<m. The bound is (mk)≤(1+ρ)mρk. {m\choose k}\le\frac{(1+\rho)^m}{\rho^k}. Differentiation with respect to log⁡ρ\log\rho gives mρ/(1+ρ)−km\rho/(1+\rho)-k. Its zero is ρ=k/(m−k)\rho=k/(m-k), so the optimized bound is (mk)≤(mk)k(mm−k)m−k. {m\choose k}\le \left(\frac{m}{k}\right)^k \left(\frac{m}{m-k}\right)^{m-k}. At m=2,k=1m=2,k=1, it gives 2≤42\le4, not an exact evaluation. The stationarity equation has balanced the expected degree with the target degree. In the face source there is one such equation for each ordinary face, coupled through all the factors containing that face. This is why a local optimum at every box is not yet a shared coefficient estimate.

Refining the coefficient estimate by genuine sectors

A single positive comparison can be too coarse even when a more detailed finite comparison succeeds. The sector-cover theorem for certified marked plats and its general Morse formulation make this precise for their stated original sources. They use the actual central, left and right polynomials, not arbitrary pieces of an upper-bound formula.

For nonnegative weights w1,w2,w3w_1,w_2,w_3, not all zero, define the source-normalized triangle potential by Q(w1,w2,w3)=maxαj≥0α1+α2+α3=1/2∑j=13(f(αj)+αjlog⁡wj),f(t)=Λ(πt)+Λ(π/2−πt)π,0≤t≤12. \begin{aligned} Q(w_1,w_2,w_3) &=\max_{\substack{\alpha_j\ge0\\ \alpha_1+\alpha_2+\alpha_3=1/2}} \sum_{j=1}^{3}\bigl(f(\alpha_j)+\alpha_j\log w_j\bigr),\\ f(t)&=\frac{\Lambda(\pi t)+\Lambda(\pi/2-\pi t)}{\pi}, \qquad 0\le t\le\tfrac12 . \end{aligned} For an ordered face quartet (x,b,c,y)(x,b,c,y), the three weights in Q(x,b,y)Q(x,b,y) are w1=x,w2=b,w3=yw_1=x,w_2=b,w_3=y. If a weight is zero, restrict its αj\alpha_j to zero and assign its product αjlog⁡wj\alpha_j\log w_j the value zero. The linked proof identifies this maximum with the limiting logarithm, divided by NN, of the original positive half-degree triangle polynomial. Thus this is a real function of face weights, not the root QN=qN2Q_N=q_N^2, and no additive normalization is free: Q(x,0,0)=12log⁡xQ(x,0,0)=\tfrac12\log x for x>0x>0. The local bounds on the entire real spectral interval have rates AC=Q(x,b,y)+Q(x,c,y),AL=max⁡{log⁡c,Q(x,b,y)+12log⁡c},AR=max⁡{log⁡b,Q(x,c,y)+12log⁡b}. \begin{aligned} A_C&=Q(x,b,y)+Q(x,c,y),\\ A_L&=\max\{\log c,\ Q(x,b,y)+\tfrac12\log c\},\\ A_R&=\max\{\log b,\ Q(x,c,y)+\tfrac12\log b\}. \end{aligned} If the overloaded middle coordinate is protected, its one-sided polynomial is exactly zero and that label is omitted. A protected zero never becomes a small positive weight. These are bounds uniform in the phases on the face coefficient torus; they do not assert coefficientwise positivity of the one-sided polynomial.

A branch BB allows a specified subset SB,eS_{B,e} of genuine labels at each crossing region. For example, one branch can require left at region 1, allow central or right at region 2, and allow all labels elsewhere. It has the shared cost GB(𝑳)=∑emaxa∈SB,eAe,a(𝑳)−∑fordinarylog⁡Lf. Every face still has just one weight within this branch. Different branches may use different full vectors of weights.

More generally a branch can be any specified finite set BB of genuine label strings. Its cost is GB(𝑳)=max𝒔∈B∑eAe,se(𝑳)−∑fordinarylog⁡Lf. G_B(\boldsymbol L) =\max_{\boldsymbol s\in B}\sum_e A_{e,s_e}(\boldsymbol L) -\sum_{f\ \mathrm{ordinary}}\log L_f. The preceding sum of local maxima is the special case where all combinations of the allowed local labels belong to BB. For example, a two-region branch containing only (L,C)(L,C) and (C,R)(C,R) has the maximum of A1,L+A2,CA_{1,L}+A_{2,C} and A1,C+A2,RA_{1,C}+A_{2,R}. Taking separate local maxima would also allow (L,R)(L,R), which is not in that branch and can have a larger bound. Retaining such correlations refines an estimate without changing the original source labels.

Choose finitely many branches covering every noncentral label string. If each has one fixed positive shared vector with GB<ΦDG_B<\Phi_D, where 2πΦD2\pi\Phi_D is the complete augmented volume, their finitely many strict gaps give a common bound on the full complementary polynomial. The original filling estimate transfers that bound to the complementary scalar. For independently sufficiently long fixed twists, the nonzero all-central contribution has a rate closer to ΦD\Phi_D than this remainder does; multiplying that rate by 2π2\pi gives the actual filled volume. When the original endpoint pairing gives a knot, this comparison proves its full volume limit under the certificate hypotheses.

An anchored test requires one specified left or right region and allows all labels at every other region. Refining that test splits its allowed strings into smaller branches. The certificate-separation example proves that all positive weights fail one anchored test on a fixed original source, while forty refined branches pass. It refutes universal applicability of the anchored test, not the volume conjecture or the possibility of a finer certificate.

Existence of a successful finite cover is a separate condition on the chosen envelopes and diagram. Refinement does not prove it: even a branch containing a single genuine string still requires one shared weight vector giving the stated strict inequality.

There is an exact obstruction at this last level. The 54-region example has one left region and central at all other regions. For this fixed string 𝒔\boldsymbol s, every positive shared vector satisfies G{𝒔}(𝑳)>ΦD+15. G_{\{\boldsymbol s\}}(\boldsymbol L)>\Phi_D+\frac15. The proof assigns rational angle loads to the original triangles so that their face totals match every original coefficient target. The logarithmic weights then cancel, leaving an entropy lower bound valid for every shared vector. A separate rational trial vector bounds the complete action from above. The shared sector is also nonzero at every sufficiently large even level, so it cannot simply be discarded as an unsupported label choice. Any branch containing this string has at least its envelope cost. Subdivision by genuine labels therefore cannot make these particular envelopes a universal criterion. This conclusion gives no lower bound for the signed coefficient or filled invariant; cancellation and stronger estimates remain possible.

This is a finite cover of a set of sector choices. It is unrelated to a finite covering space of a knot complement. The theorem also does not split the two scalar expressions inside ALA_L into separate physical sectors: both expressions bound the same left polynomial.

Why weak balance can still give a strict gap

There is a sufficient condition that constructs the shared weights directly. Let wf*>0w_f^*>0 be the ordinary curvatures of the complete marked packing, with the protected weights zero. At every region put Xe=Q(x,b,y)+Q(x,c,y)X_e=Q(x,b,y)+Q(x,c,y), evaluated at these weights. The weak-balance theorem assumes Xe≥log⁡wb*(wb*>0),Xe≥log⁡wc*(wc*>0). X_e\ge\log w_b^*\quad(w_b^*>0),\qquad X_e\ge\log w_c^*\quad(w_c^*>0). Equality is allowed. These inequalities are a geometric hypothesis, not a consequence of the definition of a matched sphere.

The proof changes only the coefficient-estimate weights: wf(t)=(wf*)1−t,0<t<1. w_f(t)=(w_f^*)^{1-t},\qquad 0<t<1. The protected weights stay zero. This new vector need not be a circle packing. It is a permitted torus of positive moduli for the same original coefficient.

Here is why a strict margin appears. Put ξf=log⁡wf*\xi_f=\log w_f^*, and let Q△(𝝃)Q_\triangle(\boldsymbol\xi) denote the triangle potential evaluated at the exponentials of its ordinary coordinates, with its protected coordinates zero. The complete shared action is 𝒫(𝝃)=∑original triangles △Q△(𝝃)−∑fordinaryξf,∇𝒫(𝝃)=0,𝒫(𝝃)=ΦD. \mathcal P(\boldsymbol\xi) =\sum_{\text{original triangles }\triangle}Q_\triangle(\boldsymbol\xi) -\sum_{f\ \mathrm{ordinary}}\xi_f, \qquad \nabla\mathcal P(\boldsymbol\xi)=0,\qquad \mathcal P(\boldsymbol\xi)=\Phi_D. For the maximizing normalized angles α△,f\alpha_{\triangle,f}, its triangle entropy is E△=Q△−∑fα△,fξf=∑ff(α△,f). E_\triangle=Q_\triangle-\sum_f\alpha_{\triangle,f}\xi_f =\sum_f f(\alpha_{\triangle,f}). It is positive when the triangle has at least two ordinary corners. Complex critical points and the contribution of the original contour

An oscillatory integral or finite sum can have several complex critical points. If its phase is F(z)F(z), a critical point solves ∇F(z)=0\nabla F(z)=0. It is nondegenerate when the Hessian matrix ∂2F/∂zi∂zj\partial^2F/\partial z_i\partial z_j is invertible. A local Gaussian calculation then involves its determinant. The determinant’s square root must follow the chosen contour and branch.

The main issue is not just finding such a point. One must move the original contour to a neighborhood of it without losing boundary contributions, prove that the resulting coefficient is nonzero, and bound the complement. In the filling calculation, Fourier expansion produces integer indices ℓe\ell_e, often called charges, in addition to the color variables. The original sum contains all those charges. A calculation for ℓe=1\ell_e=1 alone does not establish the full asymptotic.

The whole geometric-period proof treats the large charges on the entire original intervals, retaining their endpoint currents. The remaining finite charges are compared on fixed complex neighborhoods. The phases, derivatives at repeated roots, and signs of independently chosen twists remain in the formula. For the covered full-invariant theorems, a further estimate controls the terms outside this geometric sector.

The same distinction matters for slowly varying recurrences. Distinct instantaneous eigenvalues do not alone exclude exponentially significant transfer between branches. A small off-diagonal entry may be magnified after conjugation by exponential action weights. The transport counterexamples and bounds explain the precise missing estimate. Thus neither diagonalizing each matrix separately nor naming a dominant classical branch is a substitute for controlling their ordered product.

Local stationary-phase methods are treated in Hörmander, The Analysis of Linear Partial Differential Operators I. A related root-of-unity asymptotic problem is Garoufalidis and Zagier’s Nahm-sum analysis. Its fixed-root radial limit and hypotheses differ from the growing-order roots and finite knot sums here; no identification of those objects is assumed.

The classical AA-polynomial records peripheral eigenvalue pairs of representations of the knot group, after the chosen convention about reducible factors. The classical reference is Cooper, Culler, Gillet, Long, and Shalen, Plane curves associated to character varieties of 3-manifolds. The peripheral basis and the choice between eigenvalues and squared eigenvalues are part of the definition.

Torsors, logarithms, and the absolute volume

A torsor for a group GG is a nonempty space on which GG acts freely and transitively: every two points differ by a unique group element, but there is no preferred origin. For example, the two square roots of a nonzero complex number form a torsor for μ2={1,−1}\mu_2=\{1,-1\}. Choices of logarithm differ by 2πiℤ2\pi i\mathbb Z. Horosphere decorations of a fixed positive cusped structure have one positive scale freedom per cusp.

These examples are related bookkeeping problems, but they are not interchangeable torsors. The notes specify which group acts, what the points are, and which marked data choose a section. Passing to a cover can make a square root single-valued. It does not automatically choose the original quantum period or justify descent of its normalization.

For torsors in algebraic families, one must also specify the base and the topology in which local sections exist; see Stacks Project, §39.11. The elementary examples above have explicit fibers and actions.

The algebraic approaches chapter explains the proposed Hilbert 90 and Koszul constructions, the precise role of cyclotomic data, Regge symmetry and nilpotent group transport, and descent from covering spaces. It also keeps the early positive factorial model and its failed exact move test separate from its valid asymptotic calculation.

For an oriented ideal tetrahedron with shape z∈ℂ\{0,1}z\in\mathbb C\setminus\{0,1\}, the signed volume is its Bloch–Wigner dilogarithm D(z)=Im⁡Li⁡2(z)+arg⁡(1−z)log⁡|z|, D(z)=\operatorname{Im}\operatorname{Li}_2(z) +\arg(1-z)\log|z|, under its continuous single-valued interpretation. The dilogarithm initially satisfies Li⁡2(z)=∑n≥1zn/n2\operatorname{Li}_2(z)=\sum_{n\ge1}z^n/n^2 for |z|<1|z|<1, with analytic continuation elsewhere. A regulator here is the map taking the algebraic tetrahedral data, with their relations and markings, to the corresponding volume or complex period.

The volume is positive for the positive orientation Im⁡z>0\operatorname{Im}z>0. Neumann’s extended Bloch-group paper explains how logarithmic lifts enter the complex regulator. The ordinary Bloch–Wigner volume and that lifted complex quantity must be distinguished.

Algebraic gluing equations can make the selected shapes algebraic. That does not make their dilogarithm values algebraic numbers. Nor does algebraicity select an integration cycle for a quantum sum. To identify an action with volume, the proofs retain the actual shapes and based peripheral words, compare their differentials, and fix the additive constant at a known complete structure.

A period is the integral of a specified differential form over a specified cycle. A relative cycle may have boundary in a prescribed boundary set, so its endpoints are part of the data. The elementary identity ∫abdf=f(b)−f(a)\int_a^b df=f(b)-f(a) shows why an exact differential need not integrate to zero on such a cycle. Likewise, deforming a contour across a pole can change the value by a residue. The rational-period construction retains the original contour, translates it with its orientation, and uses common cutoffs around the new poles before taking the alternating sum. Specifying its algebraic integrand alone would leave out those data. Choosing a logarithm or a square-root sheet solves another part of the marking problem; it does not by itself select this relative cycle or bound its period.

For a filled auxiliary cusp, let U,VU,V be the logarithms of the similarity multipliers of its primitive meridian and longitude. Equivalently, these are twice the selected eigenvalue logarithms, with the eigenvalues divided by their complete central signs before taking logarithms. In the convention used by the general Morse theorem, filling the primitive slope μ+pλ\mu+p\lambda imposes U+pV=−2πi. U+pV=-2\pi i. The integer pp is a fixed surgery coefficient. The specified logarithm is stronger data than the exponentiated equality eU+pV=1e^{U+pV}=1. Smooth metric completion, rather than the exponentiated equation alone, identifies the resulting hyperbolic manifold. The regulator and completion proof gives this argument in detail.

The standard peripheral deformation and volume-potential framework is Neumann and Zagier, Volumes of hyperbolic three-manifolds. The signs, primitive periods, and eigenvalue-versus-ratio convention in the displayed filling equation come from the local marked proof, not from an unrecorded change of that reference’s coordinates.

The A-structure and torsor route retains this algebraic information and the selected period. Its general unresolved question is a source-preserving norm and survival theorem. An algebraic branch, a chosen logarithm, and a calibrated classical volume are substantial inputs, but they do not by themselves estimate the quantum invariant.

Changing the order in which representation factors are paired is called recoupling. Its matrix coefficients are quantum 6j6j, or Racah, coefficients. There are two substantial limits to this conclusion. First, it keeps all eight cycle projections, and it treats only odd levels. The finite comparison does not control the eight omitted sums. Second, nonzero rung twists introduce different phases into different summands. With q=eπi/Nq=e^{\pi i/N}, the sector phase of a signed crossing power ee is ωje,ωj=qd2/2(−1)jqj(j+1). \omega_j^e,\qquad \omega_j=q^{d^2/2}(-1)^j q^{j(j+1)}. Cycle twists have a common phase because all their channels are fixed at mm; rung twists generally do not. The positivity argument above therefore proves the stated zero-rung-twist packet, not noncancellation of an arbitrary twisted rung sum. The volume is that of the complement with its crossing circles retained. Neither removal of all projections nor a full filled-knot volume limit follows from this result.

Which approaches are retained, and where to read them

The table covers the organized research routes. Each route includes its exact results, examples, and limitations; the archive also preserves earlier versions and unsuccessful constructions. Historical claims should be read with their corrections and dates, not promoted to current theorems.

Approach What the argument does Current place in the project
Tangle trees and planar decoration Exact composition, strict estimates on shared variables, and a nonzero filling contribution. Full volume theorems for the stated constructions and twist conditions.
Moving the main meridian Controls the actual reduced state on microscopic meridian neighborhoods and computes its correction. Completed for the stated tree classes and domains; broader resonant continuation is separate.
Finite source and transport Preserves matrix entries, spectral derivatives, endpoint terms, and shared coefficient constraints. Exact identities and several bounds; an identity alone is not a volume theorem.
Angle optimization and geometry Constructs cells, gluing, peripheral holonomy, and smooth ends. Actual volume identification under the stated geometric hypotheses.
A-structures and torsors Retains the selected solution, algebraic branch, logarithms, and regulator normalization. Pointed and selected-factor theorems in covered families; general period estimates remain open.
Algebraic proposals and their tests Examines exact move identities, transport equations, finite complexes, root data, and descent. Exact constructions and explicit obstructions; each proposed volume consequence requires its stated additional comparison.
Global maxima of the signed source Keeps the common degree constraints while estimating possible competing regions. Partial bounds and classifications; the unrestricted signed remainder comparison is open.
Fourier, Cauchy, and energy Exact norm identities and weighted inequalities, with original boundary costs. Completed local estimates and special-family arguments; no general lower bound follows from positivity.
Cube and shared-core calculations Tests joining formulas and the extra geometry carried by a non-tree core. Proved projected statements; the full unprojected cube limit is not asserted.
Obstructions, transport, and alternative criteria Records counterexamples to shortcuts, cross-level conditions, finite-cover questions, and remaining source estimates. Research tools and precise open questions, rather than additional claimed knot families.

Finite covering spaces also occur in algebraic and transport proposals. Hyperbolic volume multiplies by the degree of a finite covering map between complete hyperbolic manifolds. The original invariant has no automatic matching covering formula. The peripheral eigenvalue map and selected state would have to be transported and descended explicitly. Such a cover is a possible construction tool, not an established reduction of the general conjecture; it is distinct from the proved finite-sector criterion above.

Literature and what is borrowed from it

The following references identify the origin and established scope of the methods. The linked repository proofs state the actual versions used here. Historical attribution, an external theorem, a local proof, and a proposed extension are different kinds of evidence.

The citations beside the methods identify the relevant statement and its role. For a first reading, the following sequence is useful:

  1. Invariant and volume question: Kashaev’s triangulated and R-matrix constructions, followed by the volume paper and Murakami–Murakami for the colored Jones relation.
  2. Geometric optimization: Rivin’s variational paper and Futer–Guéritaud for angle structures, allowed tangent directions, and the precise critical-point criterion.
  3. Norm estimates: Terras for finite Fourier analysis; Marshall–Olkin–Arnold and Horn for majorization and product singular values. Weighted Cauchy and finite Parseval are proved explicitly above.
  4. Cross-level asymptotics: Taylor’s Tauberian notes. Compare the cumulative conclusion with the additional source-transport condition in the local criterion.
  5. Recurrences: Wilf–Zeilberger for multisum elimination, then the colored-Jones and ADO papers for the objects and conventions concerned. Keep the classical A-curve and the proposed characteristic comparison separate.
  6. Regulators and peripheral geometry: Neumann–Zagier for the local deformation potential, Neumann for logarithmic regulator data, and the explicit torsor definitions for any claimed group action.
  7. Local asymptotics versus a whole knot period: Costantino’s 6j6j-volume result and the Nahm-sum comparison illustrate related problems with different normalizations and limiting regimes. The complete-source estimates and contour arguments remain the additional work in this project.

The comparison of volume statements separately records which invariant and knot or link family occur in related results. In particular, a theorem for a local recoupling coefficient, a partially projected source, or a different three-manifold invariant should not be read as the same assertion as a full Kashaev limit for the original knot.

The literature search is a coverage and attribution check. It does not turn an external paper into an unchecked proof input, or turn a local calculation into a theorem about an arbitrary knot. The manuscript states the actual hypotheses and supplies the arguments used for its conclusions.
