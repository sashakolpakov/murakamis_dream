Research guide

Algebraic approaches and the tests they must pass

The algebraic approaches in this project try to retain an entire quantum state and its relations, rather than estimate every summand separately. They ask whether gluing, changes of coordinates, and evaluation at roots of unity can determine the volume contribution. Several of these constructions give exact identities. Their implications for the growth of the original knot invariant require additional arguments, specified below.

The methods chapter introduces the finite invariant, weighted estimates, Fourier transformation, and geometric optimization. Here we explain the algebra behind some earlier proposals. This chapter also retains an unsuccessful model: knowing why it fails as an exact state is useful even when its volume rate is correct. These proposals are not premises silently added to the manuscript’s volume theorems.

A positive factorial model and a change of triangulation

Fix an integer N≥2N\ge2, and put ζN=e2πi/N,SN(k)=∏j=1k2sin⁡(πj/N),SN(0)=1. \zeta_N=e^{2\pi i/N},\qquad S_N(k)=\prod_{j=1}^k2\sin(\pi j/N),\qquad S_N(0)=1. The index satisfies 0≤k<N0\le k<N, so these products are positive. The finite quantum factorial (ζN;ζN)k=∏j=1k(1−ζNj) (\zeta_N;\zeta_N)_k=\prod_{j=1}^k(1-\zeta_N^j) has the exact expression (ζN;ζN)k=(−i)kexp⁡(πik(k+1)2N)SN(k). (\zeta_N;\zeta_N)_k =(-i)^k\exp\!\left(\frac{\pi i k(k+1)}{2N}\right)S_N(k). Indeed, each factor is 1−e2πij/N=−2ieπij/Nsin⁡(πj/N)1-e^{2\pi i j/N}=-2i\,e^{\pi i j/N}\sin(\pi j/N). Thus the positive model retains the magnitudes of familiar quantum factorials. Removing their phases changes a sum of such terms.

A triangulated model must also be compared under changes of its triangulation. In a 2↔32\leftrightarrow3 move, two tetrahedra sharing a face are replaced by three tetrahedra surrounding an interior edge. The boundary triangulation is unchanged. An exact identity must compare the two evaluations with the same boundary data and with every internal summation measure included.

In the drawing, P,QP,Q are the apices and A,B,CA,B,C the equatorial vertices. The two tetrahedra are PABCPABC and QABCQABC. The three are PQABPQAB, PQBCPQBC, and PQCAPQCA. The shaded face on the left and the highlighted edge on the right are internal.

For the symmetric test in the comparison of the custom definitions, take 6∣N6\mid N. The two bare evaluations are B2,N=SN(N/3)−6,B3,N=SN(2N/3)−3∑p=1N/3−1[SN(p)SN(N/3−p)]−3. B_{2,N}=S_N(N/3)^{-6}, \qquad B_{3,N}=S_N(2N/3)^{-3} \sum_{p=1}^{N/3-1} \bigl[S_N(p)S_N(N/3-p)\bigr]^{-3}. The sum is over the additional internal label on the three-tetrahedron side. At N=6N=6, S6(1)=1,S6(2)=3,S6(4)=6,B2,6=127,B3,6=1216. S_6(1)=1,\quad S_6(2)=\sqrt3,\quad S_6(4)=6,\quad B_{2,6}=\frac1{27},\quad B_{3,6}=\frac1{216}. The ratio is eight. More generally, the calculation in the linked note gives B2,N/B3,N∼NB_{2,N}/B_{3,N}\sim N along 6∣N6\mid N. Consequently the two expressions have the same logarithmic rate, since log⁡N/N→0\log N/N\to0, while their finite values differ. Multiplying one side by NN fixes the leading scale, but already fails to give equality at N=6N=6.

This test does not decide whether a model with additional boundary factors could satisfy an exact move law. For example, a product of three factors 1+Maj1+M^{a_j}, with integers aja_j, equals eight at M=1M=1. To use such an explanation, one must construct the generic boundary state, derive its multiplier, and verify compatibility under successive moves. A numerical ratio at one boundary value does not determine that multiplier.

The completed positive variational result is retained. Equivalence of this positive model with the original Kashaev invariant remains unproved. For the literature origin of the quantum pentagon relation, see Faddeev and Kashaev, Quantum Dilogarithm. Their operator identity and the bare positive sum above are different objects.

Hilbert 90 solves a transport equation

Let E/FE/F be a finite cyclic extension of fields, of degree rr, and let σ\sigma generate its Galois group. This means that the field automorphisms fixing FF are 1,σ,…,σr−11,\sigma,\ldots,\sigma^{r-1}, that σr=1\sigma^r=1, and that their common fixed field is FF. The notation E×E^\times means the nonzero elements of EE, under multiplication. For a∈E×a\in E^\times, its norm is Nm⁡E/F(a)=∏j=0r−1σj(a). \operatorname{Nm}_{E/F}(a)=\prod_{j=0}^{r-1}\sigma^j(a). Hilbert’s theorem 90 says that norm one is equivalent to a=bσ(b)for some b∈E×. a=\frac{b}{\sigma(b)} \quad\text{for some }b\in E^\times. The norm of such a quotient is one by cancellation of consecutive factors. The converse is the substantive assertion. See Milne, Fields and Galois Theory, version 4.22, Corollary 5.24.

This has a direct interpretation for a scalar difference equation. If σ(y)=ay\sigma(y)=a\,y and a=b/σ(b)a=b/\sigma(b), then σ(by)=by\sigma(by)=by. Multiplication by bb removes the coefficient from the equation. In this setting a gauge is precisely such an invertible change of the dependent variable. Reversing the convention for the equation reverses the quotient.

The theorem gives existence of bb; it does not estimate its absolute value under a chosen complex embedding. It also allows multiplication of bb by any element of F×F^\times. A comparison of quantum periods must retain the normalization selecting a particular gauge and control the sizes of that gauge and its inverse as NN grows.

There is an explicit warning within the original source calculations. The primitive gauge calculation works in the nilpotent algebra ℂ[z]/(zN)\mathbb C[z]/(z^N). Put QN=e2πi/NQ_N=e^{2\pi i/N}. In that algebra it constructs the unique unit gNg_N with constant term one satisfying gN(QNz)gN(z)=1(1−z)(1−QNz). \frac{g_N(Q_Nz)}{g_N(z)} =\frac1{(1-z)(1-Q_Nz)}. Both denominators are units because their constant terms are one. This is a concrete cyclic transport equation in a ring with nilpotents, not a direct application of the field theorem above. A unit is an element with a multiplicative inverse. A nilpotent element has a power equal to zero; here zN=0z^N=0. In particular (1−z)−1=1+z+⋯+zN−1(1-z)^{-1}=1+z+\cdots+z^{N-1} in this ring. Choose the unique polynomial representative of degree less than NN. On |z|≤1/4|z|\le1/4, the calculation proves gN(z)=exp⁡(2Li⁡2(z)hN+O(N−1)),hN=2πiN. g_N(z)= \exp\!\left(\frac{2\operatorname{Li}_2(z)}{h_N} +O(N^{-1})\right), \qquad h_N=\frac{2\pi i}{N}. Here Li⁡2(z)=∑k≥1zk/k2\operatorname{Li}_2(z)=\sum_{k\ge1}z^k/k^2 on this disk. The proof estimates the finite polynomial itself, including its truncation error. For complex zz, the real part of its leading exponent can have size proportional to NN. Exact trivialization of the transport can therefore carry an exponential cost.

The polynomial companion construction provides another exact change of coordinates, retaining the original input vector and output row. Its residual spectral polynomial has not thereby been identified with the knot’s geometric A-polynomial. Both constructions are useful algebraic results with stated remaining comparison problems.

Why a Koszul complex appears

Suppose RR is a commutative ring of coefficients and the gluing conditions are H1=⋯=Hs=1H_1=\cdots=H_s=1, where Hi∈RH_i\in R. Write fi=1−Hif_i=1-H_i. The quotient R/(f1,…,fs)R/(f_1,\ldots,f_s) imposes these equations. A Koszul complex also records relations between the equations.

A chain complex has modules CiC_i and maps di:Ci→Ci−1d_i:C_i\to C_{i-1} with di−1di=0d_{i-1}d_i=0. Its homology Hi(C)=ker⁡di/im⁡di+1H_i(C)=\ker d_i/\operatorname{im}d_{i+1} records elements killed by one map, modulo those already supplied by the preceding map. An RR-module allows addition and multiplication by coefficients in RR, as a vector space does over a field. Below RkR^k is the free module of kk-tuples.

For one equation its entire definition is the two-term sequence 0→R→f1R→0. 0\longrightarrow R \xrightarrow{\ f_1\ } R\longrightarrow0. For two equations it is 0→R→c↦(−f2c,f1c)R2→(a,b)↦f1a+f2bR→0. 0\longrightarrow R \xrightarrow{\ c\mapsto(-f_2c,f_1c)\ } R^2 \xrightarrow{\ (a,b)\mapsto f_1a+f_2b\ }R \longrightarrow0. The composite map is zero. Its degree-zero homology is R/(f1,f2)R/(f_1,f_2). The degree-one homology is the set of relations f1a+f2b=0f_1a+f_2b=0, modulo the relations (a,b)=(−f2c,f1c)(a,b)=(-f_2c,f_1c) already supplied by the first map. For ss equations one takes the tensor product of the corresponding two-term complexes. The sign rule is d(x⊗y)=d(x)⊗y+(−1)px⊗d(y),x∈Cp. d(x\otimes y)=d(x)\otimes y+(-1)^p x\otimes d(y), \qquad x\in C_p. It makes the two mixed terms in d2d^2 cancel. This is the standard Koszul construction.

There is a particularly concrete reason for it in the finite translation calculation. Let VV have basis e0,…,en−1e_0,\ldots,e_{n-1}, and define Tej=ej+1(j<n−1),Ten−1=He0. Te_j=e_{j+1}\quad(j<n-1),\qquad Te_{n-1}=H e_0. Then Tn=HIT^n=H I. The cokernel of 1−T1-T identifies all the eje_j, and its final relation is (1−H)e0=0(1-H)e_0=0. More precisely, separate changes of basis in the domain and codomain, using no division, reduce 1−T1-T to diag⁡(1,…,1,1−H). \operatorname{diag}(1,\ldots,1,1-H). The finite translation proof gives those bases explicitly. This is a reduction of a linear map, not conjugation of TT by one basis change. Each diagonal entry one gives a two-term complex R→1RR\xrightarrow{1}R, whose homology is zero. It can be removed by an explicit contracting homotopy: send its degree-zero copy of RR back to degree one by the identity map hh, and set h=0h=0 elsewhere. Then dh+hd=1dh+hd=1. This identity supplies the contraction, rather than merely observing that the quotient is zero. The remaining complex is R→1−HRR\xrightarrow{1-H}R. For several commuting translations on separate tensor factors, tensoring these reductions gives a chain equivalence with the Koszul complex of the equations 1−Hi1-H_i. It preserves homology without asserting that the original and reduced complexes have equal dimensions.

A sequence is regular if its generated ideal is proper and each fif_i is a non-zero-divisor after the preceding equations have been imposed: multiplication by fif_i is injective on R/(f1,…,fi−1)R/(f_1,\ldots,f_{i-1}). For such a sequence the higher Koszul homology vanishes; see Stacks Project, Lemma 15.31.2. With redundant equations it can remain. For example, in R=ℚ[x]R=\mathbb Q[x], the sequence (x,x)(x,x) has degree-one homology R/(x)R/(x): every relation is (a,−a)(a,-a), and the boundaries are exactly those with aa divisible by xx. By contrast, the single equation x2x^2 has zero higher homology although its quotient has a nilpotent element. Singularity or nonreducedness alone does not establish a failure of Koszul exactness.

This construction describes simultaneous algebraic constraints. It does not select an integration cycle, prove a nonzero quantum evaluation, or determine a regulator constant. Those are additional data and arguments.

What cyclotomic rigidity can determine

The elementary model of a rigidity argument is a polynomial P(q)∈ℚ[q]P(q)\in\mathbb Q[q] which vanishes at infinitely many distinct roots of unity. It must be zero: a nonzero polynomial has at most its degree many roots. The polynomial is fixed throughout this argument.

Compare the level-dependent polynomials PN(q)=qN−1P_N(q)=q^N-1. Each vanishes at ζN\zeta_N, but none is the zero polynomial. Moreover, qddqPN(q)|q=ζN=N. \left.q\frac{d}{dq}P_N(q)\right|_{q=\zeta_N}=N. Thus knowing the root values does not give their transverse derivatives. A jet of order kk at a point means its Taylor coefficients through order kk, or equivalently its derivatives of orders 0,…,k0,\ldots,k. Values are only jets of order zero. The cyclotomic polynomial Φn(q)\Phi_n(q) is the monic polynomial whose roots are the primitive nn-th roots of unity. In characteristic zero, divisibility by Φn(q)k+1\Phi_n(q)^{k+1} means vanishing of all these derivatives through order kk at every primitive nn-th root.

There is also an established uniqueness theorem for a different, precisely specified class. Habiro’s cyclotomic completion is ℤ[q]̂=lim←nℤ[q]/In,In=((1−q)(1−q2)⋯(1−qn)). \widehat{\mathbb Z[q]} =\underset{n}{\lim_{\leftarrow}}\mathbb Z[q]/I_n,\qquad I_n=\bigl((1-q)(1-q^2)\cdots(1-q^n)\bigr). An element is a compatible system of polynomial residue classes, not an arbitrary list of values at different levels. Each root of unity gives an evaluation because the displayed product eventually vanishes there. The full Taylor series at one root determines an element of this completion; its values at all roots also determine it. These are injectivity statements in Habiro, Cyclotomic completions of polynomial rings, Theorems 5.4 and 6.3. The integer coefficient ring and the specified completion matter. These statements neither put an arbitrary level-dependent state in that completion nor estimate its size. A proposed regulator comparison would first have to be constructed in the same class.

The whole algebraic comparison theorem works with a fixed algebraic presentation, bounds on its possible comparison maps, and the required quantum jets. Its reconstruction argument uses separation of exponents and finite linear algebra. The rank-one descent theorem likewise has a fixed rational coefficient and whole cyclic transport identities as hypotheses. Neither theorem manufactures an algebraic comparison with the geometric period from isolated numerical values.

The geometric distinction is equally important. Nondegenerate gluing equations with a fixed meridian can select algebraic tetrahedron shapes z1,…,ztz_1,\ldots,z_t. If these shapes realize the specified oriented hyperbolic triangulation of YY, with its required peripheral completion, its volume is the regulator value Vol⁡(Y)=∑j=1tD(zj),D(z)=Im⁡Li⁡2(z)+log⁡|z|arg⁡(1−z), \operatorname{Vol}(Y)=\sum_{j=1}^t D(z_j),\qquad D(z)=\operatorname{Im}\operatorname{Li}_2(z) +\log|z|\arg(1-z), with the orientations retained and the continuous Bloch–Wigner interpretation of DD. An arbitrary algebraic gluing solution has not yet been shown to realize that metric. Algebraicity of the shapes does not make this dilogarithm evaluation algebraic. More importantly, it does not show that the original finite quantum evaluation has this exponential rate.

The selected-period analysis tests exactly this distinction. In the figure-eight example, the same state has exponential growth at the complete meridian and bounded values on fixed ordinary-color sequences approaching that meridian. A proposed uniform comparison must retain the derivative data and the correct domain. Uniqueness of an algebraically specified object does not prove that a proposed regulator period satisfies its equations.

Regge symmetry and nilpotent transport

A Regge involution fixes a pair of opposite tetrahedral edges. If the other four edge lengths are a,b,c,da,b,c,d, put s=(a+b+c+d)/2s=(a+b+c+d)/2 and replace them by s−a,s−b,s−c,s−d. s-a,\quad s-b,\quad s-c,\quad s-d. For a tetrahedron in Euclidean, spherical, or hyperbolic space, the geometric Regge theorem identifies the transformed dihedral angles and proves equality of volume; see Akopyan and Izmestiev, The Regge symmetry, confocal conics, and the Schläfli formula, Theorem 1.1. This is a specific transformation of one tetrahedron, not an arbitrary change of a knot’s triangulation. At ideal vertices the edge lengths are infinite, so the ideal discussion below uses angles and logarithmic shapes instead of substituting into this finite-length formula.

For an ideal tetrahedron, opposite dihedral angles agree. The angle data are a triple (α,β,γ)(\alpha,\beta,\gamma) with sum π\pi. The Regge and period calculation shows that the faithful finite action on these angle data reduces to their permutations. Logarithms of the shapes retain additional translations and a central period coordinate.

The elementary model for the latter is the Heisenberg group H(a,b,c)=(1ac01b001). H(a,b,c)= \begin{pmatrix}1&a&c\\0&1&b\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}. Multiplication gives H(a,b,c)H(a′,b′,c′)=H(a+a′,b+b′,c+c′+ab′). H(a,b,c)H(a',b',c') =H(a+a',b+b',c+c'+ab'). Every commutator has a=b=0a=b=0, so it is central; commutators with a further group element are the identity. This is the meaning of two-step nilpotent. The linked note constructs a specific lattice of this type for the lifted classical period, together with the finite permutation action. The full group need not be nilpotent merely because a finite-index subgroup is.

Nilpotence can simplify a differential equation. If A(t)A(t) is a strictly upper-triangular 3×33\times3 matrix and U′=A(t)UU'=A(t)U, U(0)=IU(0)=I, then products of three such matrices vanish. The exact solution at t=1t=1 is U(1)=I+∫01A(t)dt+∫0<s<t<1A(t)A(s)dsdt. U(1)=I+\int_0^1A(t)\,dt +\int_{0<s<t<1}A(t)A(s)\,ds\,dt . Differentiating the analogous formula with upper endpoint tt verifies the equation. The integrals themselves can still be transcendental. The classical logarithmic construction therefore offers a tractable transport problem, but it does not place the entire quantum knot state in this three-dimensional system. That further representation and its selected solution have to be constructed.

Passing to a cover and descending

Two different kinds of cover occur. Labeling the vertices of a tetrahedron removes a finite ordering ambiguity in its shape. A finite covering map p:Y′→Yp:Y'\to Y of hyperbolic manifolds changes the manifold. These operations have different consequences.

In the ideal-shape calculation, reordering four vertices gives six possible cross-ratios, z,11−z,z−1z,1−z,1z,zz−1. z,\quad\frac1{1-z},\quad\frac{z-1}{z}, \qquad 1-z,\quad\frac1z,\quad\frac{z}{z-1}. The twenty-four vertex orderings give each cross-ratio four times. Away from shapes with a stabilizer, the six cross-ratios form a sixfold cover of the unordered shape space. At a symmetric shape, including the regular ideal tetrahedron, some of them coincide: the stabilizer and the chosen logarithmic data must still be recorded. Orientation matters even away from those points. The first three Bloch–Wigner values equal D(z)D(z), and the last three equal −D(z)-D(z), so their unweighted sum is zero. Retaining the orientation sign is necessary to descend the volume; simply averaging over the orderings would erase it. The explicit period calculation derives these signs and the logarithmic transformations.

For an orientation-preserving manifold cover of degree DD, the pulled-back metric gives Vol⁡(Y′)=DVol⁡(Y). \operatorname{Vol}(Y') =D\,\operatorname{Vol}(Y). Choose a torus boundary component upstairs and compatible peripheral bases. Suppose its meridian and longitude map to μ′=μaλb,λ′=μcλd,ad−bc>0. \mu'=\mu^a\lambda^b,\qquad \lambda'=\mu^c\lambda^d,\qquad ad-bc>0 . For simultaneously selected eigenvalues of a restricted representation, this gives the monomial map M′=MaLb,L′=McLd. M'=M^aL^b,\qquad L'=M^cL^d. The determinant is the degree of that boundary-torus cover. It need not equal DD when there are several boundary components upstairs.

The cover calculation uses these equations to eliminate the downstairs variables from a marked geometric A-curve. It keeps track of possible multiplicities and the distinction between one and several cusps. Representations upstairs need not extend downstairs, so this restriction construction alone does not recover every component of the upstairs character variety.

Descent of the quantum invariant needs more: a relation between the actual finite states, their boundary evaluations, and their normalizations. Volume multiplication supplies no such relation. A cover can simplify the classical geometry or remove a branch ambiguity while leaving this quantum comparison unresolved.
