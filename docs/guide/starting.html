Research guide

From a knot diagram to a volume limit

This chapter introduces the objects used in the guide. It explains what is being constructed, what is being summed, and what a volume theorem must prove. The results chapter then states the families covered by the arguments. The methods chapter develops the estimates and the alternative approaches.

Knots, complements, and tangles

Definition (knot and exterior). A knot KK is a smoothly embedded circle in the three-sphere S3S^3, considered up to ambient isotopy. An ambient isotopy moves the circle continuously through embeddings; it does not let one strand pass through another. Removing the interior of a small solid-torus neighborhood of KK gives its compact exterior XKX_K. Its boundary is a torus. The interior of XKX_K is homeomorphic to S3\KS^3\setminus K, the knot complement.

A link has several circle components. Its exterior has one boundary torus for each component. This distinction matters whenever a tangle construction is closed: a well-defined scalar for a link does not make that link a knot.

Definition (hyperbolic knot). A knot is hyperbolic when S3\KS^3\setminus K admits a complete metric of constant curvature −1-1 and finite volume. The notation Vol⁡(S3\K)\operatorname{Vol}(S^3\setminus K) means the volume of this metric. The metric has an end, or cusp, corresponding to the removed knot. The knot itself is not a subset of hyperbolic space whose volume is being measured.

Definition (two-string tangle). Take two disjoint arcs properly embedded in a three-ball, with their four endpoints at four specified points on its boundary. Properly embedded means that only the endpoints meet the boundary. Such a pair is a two-string tangle. We draw the ball as a box with two upper and two lower endpoints. The endpoint positions and their order are part of the description.

Stacking two boxes joins the lower endpoints of the upper box to the upper endpoints of the lower box. A quarter-turn rotates a box and its endpoints into the new input and output positions. A closure joins the four remaining endpoints by specified arcs outside the box. Different closures can give different links.

A rational tangle is obtained from the trivial pair of arcs by a finite sequence of twists of adjacent boundary endpoints. Conway’s algebraic, or arborescent, constructions compose rational tangles along a tree of such operations. In this project “algebraic knot” has this tangle meaning. A convenient reference for the basic conventions and rational tangle classification is Kauffman and Lambropoulou, On the classification of rational tangles. The repository’s particular stacking, turning, and closing conventions are specified in Chapter 1 of the manuscript.

Following the arcs gives a finite test for the number of components. This test must count circles already closed inside an intermediate box as well as components formed by the final closure. The knot hypotheses in the theorems refer to that full test.

Half twists, full twists, and auxiliary circles

Write RR for the chosen positive crossing of two strands, including the interchange of their endpoints. The tangle RnR^n has signed half-twist count n∈ℤn\in\mathbb Z. Its inverse crossing is R−1R^{-1}. Two successive crossings form a full twist. Thus changing nn by an even integer preserves the endpoint permutation; changing its parity interchanges the two endpoints.

This is why the theorems separate the parity pattern from the long twist parameters. A parity pattern can ensure that the strands form one knot. Increasing the number of full twists then preserves that pairing, although it generally changes the knot type.

A crossing circle is an auxiliary circle bounding a disk pierced by the two strands in a twist region. To augment a diagram, remove its full twists, keep the specified residual half twist, and add the crossing circle. Do this at every designated region. The result is an augmented link with auxiliary components as well as the main strands.

Definition (Dehn filling). On one boundary torus of a link exterior, choose a primitive closed curve, called a slope. Glue in a solid torus so that this curve bounds a meridian disk of the solid torus. This operation is Dehn filling. “Primitive” means that the curve represents an indivisible element of the integral first homology of the boundary torus.

For a crossing circle, choose the meridian μe\mu_e and disk longitude λe\lambda_e with the orientations used in the proof. The subscript ee labels the twist region. In the manuscript’s convention, filling the slope μe+peλe\mu_e+p_e\lambda_e restores the signed count ne=ϵe−2pe, n_e=\epsilon_e-2p_e, where pe∈ℤp_e\in\mathbb Z and the retained half twist ϵe∈{−1,0,1}\epsilon_e\in\{-1,0,1\} is fixed in advance. The minus sign records the chosen disk orientation; it is not a universal sign convention for every diagram.

Only these auxiliary tori are filled in the knot-volume theorems. The main knot cusp remains complete and unfilled. There are therefore two different volumes during the argument: Vol⁡(YD)andVol⁡(S3\K𝒑). \operatorname{Vol}(Y_D) \quad\text{and}\quad \operatorname{Vol}(S^3\setminus K_{\boldsymbol p}). Here DD is the fixed augmented diagram, YDY_D is its link complement, and K𝒑K_{\boldsymbol p} is the knot after the auxiliary fillings. The geometric proof must reach the second volume.

What the finite invariant contains

At level N≥2N\ge2, put qN=eπi/N,QN=qN2=e2πi/N. q_N=e^{\pi i/N},\qquad Q_N=q_N^2=e^{2\pi i/N}. In the finite representation used here a main strand carries an NN-dimensional complex vector space VNV_N, with a chosen basis v0,…,vN−1v_0,\ldots,v_{N-1}. A crossing acts on VN⊗VNV_N\otimes V_N. A cup creates two endpoints, a cap joins them, and each internal wire means summation over its basis index. These maps, the closing correction, the framing, and the reduced normalization together specify the scalar KN(K)K_N(K).

The word color specifies a representation carried by a strand or auxiliary circle. Complete color in this guide means the NN-dimensional representation. An internal basis index is not a second color.

Here is the elementary matrix rule behind a stacked tangle. If AA and BB act on VN⊗VNV_N\otimes V_N, write A(vi⊗vj)=∑a,b=0N−1Aijabva⊗vb. A(v_i\otimes v_j) =\sum_{a,b=0}^{N-1}A^{ab}_{ij}\,v_a\otimes v_b. Then BB followed by AA has entries (AB)cdab=∑i,j=0N−1AijabBcdij. (AB)^{ab}_{cd} =\sum_{i,j=0}^{N-1}A^{ab}_{ij}B^{ij}_{cd}. The four indices a,b,c,da,b,c,d remain on the outside. The two internal indices i,ji,j are summed, with the same values used in both factors.

The actual crossing matrices, cup and cap entries, and closing functional appear in Chapter 2 of the manuscript. Kashaev’s original matrix construction is the starting definition. The normalization comparison in Appendix A is a separate finite calculation. An arbitrary choice of local matrices does not define a knot invariant merely because it has an interesting large-NN limit.

The source of a calculation means this specified finite expression: its local maps, summation ranges, closure, and all scalar factors. An exact source identity rewrites that expression without changing its value. The guide uses “source” in this concrete sense.

Why polynomial coefficients appear

Polynomials can record the entries of a finite tensor contraction. For instance, fix a nonnegative integer dd and write A(z)=∑j=0dajzj,B(z)=∑j=0dbjzj, A(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{d}a_jz^j,\qquad B(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{d}b_jz^j, then [zd]A(z)B(z)=∑j=0dajbd−j. [z^d]A(z)B(z) =\sum_{j=0}^{d}a_jb_{d-j}. The notation [zd][z^d] asks for the coefficient of zdz^d. It enforces one shared constraint on the two indices.

In the face description of a knot diagram, each projection region has one variable LfL_f. The subscript ff labels the region. Every local factor touching that region uses that same variable. A product of local polynomials followed by a specified coefficient extraction then enforces all face constraints at once. In the marked construction two designated face variables are set to zero; these are the protected faces. Their zero exponents come from the original cut, not from a later choice of an estimate.

For positive real numbers r1,…,rsr_1,\ldots,r_s, the elementary multivariable Cauchy formula gives |[z1k1⋯zsks]P|≤sup|zf|=rf|P(z1,…,zs)|∏f=1srfkf. |[z_1^{k_1}\cdots z_s^{k_s}]P| \le \frac{\displaystyle\sup_{|z_f|=r_f}|P(z_1,\ldots,z_s)|} {\displaystyle\prod_{f=1}^{s}r_f^{k_f}}. Here PP is a polynomial and the kfk_f are nonnegative integer target exponents. To see the formula, integrate P(r1eiθ1,…,rseiθs)e−i∑fkfθfP(r_1e^{i\theta_1},\ldots,r_se^{i\theta_s}) e^{-i\sum_f k_f\theta_f} over all θf∈[0,2π]\theta_f\in[0,2\pi], divide by (2π)s∏frfkf(2\pi)^s\prod_f r_f^{k_f}, and use Fourier orthogonality. Taking the absolute value gives the inequality.

Each occurrence of a face must have the same radius in this integral. That requirement is what the phrase “shared radii” means. When the geometric construction interprets LfL_f as a circle curvature, its circle radius is 1/Lf1/L_f. The integration radius rfr_f is a weight in a coefficient estimate; the two meanings of “radius” should not be confused.

What has to converge

Kashaev’s volume question asks for the exponential rate of the whole scalar: limN→∞2πNlog⁡|KN(K)|=Vol⁡(S3\K). \lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{2\pi}{N}\log|K_N(K)| =\operatorname{Vol}(S^3\setminus K). Its formulation comes from Kashaev’s volume paper. The Murakami–Murakami relation to colored Jones polynomials gives another formulation of the invariant. It does not by itself prove the volume limit for a new knot.

The quantifiers in a long-twist theorem have the following order:

  1. Choose one permitted finite diagram and any finite decoration required by the theorem.
  2. Find a threshold TT for that diagram.
  3. Choose every filling integer pep_e, with |pe|>T|p_e|>T, and fix these integers permanently.
  4. Let NN tend to infinity for the resulting fixed knot.

Allowing arbitrarily many tangles means that step 1 permits diagrams of arbitrarily large finite size. It does not let the number of tangles increase during step 4. A degree-and-twist criterion may give an explicit TT; an existence theorem need not do so.

To understand the analytic issue, consider ZN=eN+(−eN+1)=1. Z_N=e^N+\bigl(-e^N+1\bigr)=1. Both summands have exponential size, but their sum does not. Thus finding a critical point with the expected volume, or bounding a positive sum of absolute values, is only part of an argument.

A successful decomposition writes KN=GN+RNK_N=G_N+R_N, where GNG_N is a specified contribution and RNR_N is the remaining sum. For example, let S∈ℂS\in\mathbb C and suppose GN=aNeNS,log⁡|aN|N→0,|RN|≤eN(Re⁡S−η)+o(N),η>0. G_N=a_Ne^{NS},\qquad \frac{\log|a_N|}{N}\longrightarrow0,\qquad |R_N|\le e^{N(\operatorname{Re}S-\eta)+o(N)},\quad\eta>0. The amplitude aNa_N is nonzero for sufficiently large NN. The notation o(N)o(N) denotes an error whose ratio to NN tends to zero. These assumptions imply RN/GN→0R_N/G_N\to0, so the full scalar has rate 2πRe⁡S2\pi\operatorname{Re}S. A separate geometric argument must show that this number is the volume of the intended knot complement.

This explains the different conclusions found in the guide:

Result What it establishes
Finite identity Two expressions evaluate to the same number at each level.
Upper estimate The specified quantity cannot grow faster than the stated rate.
Nonzero geometric contribution One specified part has the stated nonzero asymptotic.
Full volume equality The entire invariant has the rate of the actual hyperbolic knot complement.

The manuscript includes completed arguments at these different levels of strength. In particular, its geometric-contribution theorem for an unchanged matched spherical diagram remains a partial result. The methods chapter explains which techniques supply each step, and the results chapter records the exact hypotheses under which all steps have been completed.
