Research guide

Why the limiting action is the actual knot volume

The volume theorem has two geometric tasks. First, construct the hyperbolic metric on the complement of the link drawn by the specified diagram. Second, identify the normalized source action with that metric’s volume. Face labels, orientations and peripheral curves enter both tasks.

Throughout this chapter DD is a fixed diagram. Its augmented exterior YDY_D is obtained by surrounding each recorded twist region with an auxiliary crossing circle and retaining its specified half twist. Filling those auxiliary cusps with the recorded integer slopes restores the signed twist powers and produces the filled exterior S3\K𝒑S^3\setminus K_{\boldsymbol p} when the main strands form a knot. The knot cusp is left unfilled. The complete augmented action is ΦD=Vol⁡(YD)2π. \Phi_D=\frac{\operatorname{Vol}(Y_D)}{2\pi}. A filled quantum asymptotic instead uses the generally smaller value Vol⁡(S3\K𝒑)/(2π)\operatorname{Vol}(S^3\setminus K_{\boldsymbol p})/(2\pi). Here NN denotes the quantum level; all diagram and filling data are fixed before NN tends to infinity.

For the strict Conway tree theorem, the geometric construction, nonzero quantum contribution and bound on the remaining terms are all proved. The planar constructions below also establish their specified complements and marked deformations. For a general planar source, proving that the complementary terms are smaller than the geometric contribution remains a separate estimate. The results and frontier chapters keep those scopes distinct.

The geometric objects and the required checks

Start with the augmented diagram: each twist region has an auxiliary crossing circle around its two strands. Cutting along the projection sphere and the original crossing disks divides its exterior into pieces with labeled faces. The labels remember exactly how to glue the pieces back. They also remember which curves on each torus end are the original meridian and longitude.

An ideal vertex lies at the boundary at infinity of hyperbolic space. A small horosphere about it cuts a Euclidean polygon from each adjacent cell. These polygons are the vertex links. In a complete link complement, the polygons around each end glue to a Euclidean torus. This torus is a cross-section of a cusp, the noncompact end of the manifold. Checking only that the dihedral angles sum to 2π2\pi does not establish cusp completeness; the horospherical polygons must also match with the correct translations.

An angle structure assigns positive dihedral angles to the proposed cells while satisfying their internal angle relations and the sum around every glued edge. Its volume is the sum of the cell volumes. The angles still retain the actual incidence equations: two copies of one manifold edge are not independent variables. At an interior maximum, variation in every allowed direction gives the shape-matching equations. The proof must then show that these equations produce the original face pairings and complete ends.

A marked construction retains the chosen peripheral curves and their basepaths. This matters after filling. The equation that closes a meridian disk depends on a primitive pair of curves on the torus, not just on an unlabeled complex cusp shape. Replacing a primitive longitude by twice that curve would change the filling problem.

The circle packing for a general planar core

A simple white triangulation is a triangulation of the projection sphere with no loop or repeated edge. Its vertices label white faces of the cut diagram. A perfect matching of the cubic dual pairs its triangles into crossing disks; the cyclic orders record the four surrounding strand ends. This matching and the chosen half twists determine which main strands join, so the knot condition is a separate component test.

The planar decoration theorem constructs the geometry of every such labeled simple sphere. Remove one triangle and prescribe three mutually tangent boundary circles. Maximize the concave angle function subject to angle sum π\pi in each triangle and 2π2\pi at each interior vertex. The proof establishes strict feasibility by a finite counting argument, so the maximum has positive angles. Its multipliers give positive circle radii.

Euclidean triangles with side lengths equal to sums of adjacent radii then develop into one embedded disk. Their angle sums remove interior holonomy, and the convex triangular boundary gives degree one. This proves that the circles have precisely the prescribed contacts. The white-circle planes and the circles through the three contact points of each triangle bound the required convex ideal right-angled polyhedron.

A terminal insertion adds the circle in one triangular gap. All old circles remain fixed. Its three new supporting disks lie inside the old shaded supporting disk, so the attached octahedron respects every other face inequality. Repeating the insertion preserves convexity, even when the original sphere has separating triangles or the tangle connections have cycles. The two projection copies recover the actual augmented link, with the original crossing-disk face maps.

For independently sufficiently long fixed fillings, the complete metric is the starting point for the deformation. At circle ee, let μe\mu_e be the disk-framed meridian and λe\lambda_e its complementary primitive longitude. The logarithms Ue,VeU_e,V_e are logarithms of their projective similarity multipliers, with the branches continued from the complete structure. They are twice logarithms of the corresponding determinant-one eigenvalues. The selected peripheral logarithms satisfy the primitive equation Ue+peVe=−2πiU_e+p_eV_e=-2\pi i. The logarithmic cusp development has angular period 2π2\pi, so its metric completion is a smooth solid torus on that slope. The main meridian and longitude remain parabolic and the knot cusp is complete. Thus the final volume is that of the knot complement; it is generally smaller than the augmented volume.

Filling conditions checked at individual circles

For the ordered tree with one crossing circle at every vertex, let dvd_v count the incident octahedral pieces in one projection half and let nvn_v be the signed crossing power. The geometric criterion dv2+nv2≥630 \sqrt{d_v^2+n_v^2}\ge630 at each vertex constructs a positive deformation through the full filling path. The contraction bound is independent of the number of vertices. The original primitive filling curve has logarithmic period −2πic-2\pi i c, for 0≤c≤10\le c\le1. At c=1c=1 it makes exactly one full turn, and the endpoint is the smooth metric on the original knot complement. The main knot cusp remains complete.

The volume decreases along this path by the Schläfli formula. Its constant is fixed at the complete augmented exterior, which has two regular ideal octahedra per tree edge. This proves that the continued action is the actual hyperbolic volume. To obtain a quantum volume equality one also needs the finite-sum estimate; the directed subtree theorem supplies it under its stronger finite numerical test. In particular, it suffices that every vertex independently satisfy |nv|≥227|n_v|\ge2^{27} or dv≥228d_v\ge2^{28}, with the original knot pairing. This proves the actual complement-volume equality. The summed criterion remains a useful alternative. The geometric bound 630630 alone is not a quantum estimate.

The geometric proof for the explicit degree-and-twist bound

The quantitative theorem uses a particularly simple decomposition of the actual exterior. Each vertex of its ordered tree is a crossing circle, and each tree edge supplies one octahedron in each projection half. Inserting a terminal child attaches an octahedron along a shaded crossing-disk triangle. Reading the four strand joins shows that its other three shaded faces are exactly the new disk faces. The adjacent white projection faces join in their original order.

There are EE octahedra in each half for a tree with EE edges. The ordered decomposition proof retains the left and right insertion order, the actual rotations of child balls, and the single original half twist at each circle. These data determine the original manifold and its primitive cusp curves.

The angular formulas subdivide the pair of octahedra into eight ideal tetrahedra. All shapes are ii at the complete point and remain positively oriented for |τv|<10−2|\tau_v|<10^{-2}. The numerical bound of the theorem puts the entire path of filling equations inside this chart. Along the path, the actual primitive slope has logarithmic period −2πic-2\pi i c, with 0≤c≤10\le c\le1. Its angular period is therefore 2πc2\pi c.

The completion argument proves that each auxiliary end is a punctured metric tube with these periods. At c=1c=1, its core is smooth. Attaching these cores performs the original surgeries, while the main cusp remains complete. The boundary correction to the complex action cancels the filling term; the resulting real value is the volume of this knot complement. The constant is fixed by the complete augmented link, whose volume is 2Ev82E\,v_8, where v8v_8 is the volume of a regular ideal octahedron.

This argument allows the full meridian logarithm to accumulate one turn even when each angular variable is small. It is the step needed for the short powers permitted by the criterion. The main-cusp continuation then compares the marked representations and follows the preferred longitude to the smooth endpoint.

The strict-tree construction

The net-twist reduction extends the use of this construction. It combines specified twist boxes by actual isotopies, retaining any half-turn of an intervening tangle. When its output is a strict presentation with sufficiently large combined twists, the geometric theorem below gives the output knot exterior its complete metric. The isotopy identifies that exterior with the input knot exterior, so the metric and its volume transfer directly. This comparison is made after the twists are inserted; it does not identify the auxiliary crossing-circle cusps of two different augmented presentations.

A Conway expression is a recorded sequence of two operations on tangles: stack two boxes, or turn one box through a quarter-turn. Its leaves are the original twist regions. The finite strictness test in the theorem is a compatibility test for the positive angles of these operations. It is not a claim that any written tangle expression becomes hyperbolic when its displayed exponents are large.

The geometric pieces follow these operations. Each leaf contributes an ideal quadrangular pyramid, and each retained product contributes an ideal triangular prism. Their volumes agree with the local terms of the complete quantum action. Each quadrilateral gluing face has four ordered ideal vertices. The order specifies which vertex is identified with which when two cells are attached; it is part of the geometry, not just a label for a boundary variable. The metrics of these faces are determined by derivatives of the action. When the full action is stationary under the shared-index constraints, those metrics agree across the actual ordered product faces.

The general strict-mask geometry theorem proves that these pieces assemble with the original cyclic orders, including turns and the different boundary edge types. An edge type records whether its face is supplied directly by a leaf crossing disk or by the surrounding projection region. The bookkeeping is needed to prevent a formally matching quadrilateral from being attached to the wrong side. The proof rules out the vertex identifications that would collapse a shaded triangle, checks the edge cycles, and constructs positive rectangular cusp links.

The final identification with the diagram uses the literal bowtie cut. A bowtie is the pair of triangular faces exposed by one crossing disk. Its four outer corners are joined by the actual main strands of the diagram. Reading those joins in their projection order determines every remaining face. Reversing the cut therefore recovers the original link exterior with its original auxiliary circles. Matching a count of vertices or a total volume would not suffice.

The marked deformation theorem for the full strict class extends the complete construction to nearby complex auxiliary colors. Its face maps satisfy the original group relations, including their translational parts. Every main cusp remains parabolic. On an auxiliary cusp it supplies specified logarithms Ui,ViU_i,V_i of the peripheral multipliers. Here a logarithm includes its chosen branch, so it records a winding that a bare eigenvalue would forget.

Write the actual twist weight as ni=ϵi−2pin_i=\epsilon_i-2p_i, with ϵi∈{0,±1}\epsilon_i\in\{0,\pm1\} recording a retained half twist. The filling equation uses the primitive basis of this actual half-twisted link. For every fixed choice of sufficiently large independent signed pip_i, the nearby solution gives a smooth filling of the auxiliary cusp. The main knot cusp is never filled. The action, with its boundary correction, equals the volume of that resulting knot complement. The quantum theorem uses exactly this solution and normalization.

The peripheral deformation viewpoint has its classical foundation in Neumann and Zagier, Volumes of hyperbolic three-manifolds. The diagram-specific work here determines the primitive words, the factor of two between multipliers and eigenvalues, and the absolute constant of the source action. Those choices are retained when applying the filling equations.

The next sections give further details and explain the older constructions that supplied parts of this argument.

Algebraic shapes and their volume regulator

The algebraic optimizer theorem applies to every rational affine ideal-angle problem with positive rational weights. On the smallest face containing the maxima, flat atoms contribute zero. The remaining Hessian is definite after quotienting by the rational directions that leave all nonflat angles unchanged. Exponentiated stationarity then proves that the nonflat shapes are algebraic, including boundary and nonunique maxima.

The Bloch group packages algebraic tetrahedron shapes together with the relations obtained by changing a triangulation. Its regulator evaluates those shape symbols by the oriented dilogarithm volume. For a shape z∉{0,1}z\notin\{0,1\}, the Bloch–Wigner dilogarithm gives the signed volume of the corresponding ideal tetrahedron. A sum of shape symbols records the tetrahedra with their orientations; the gluing relations remove its boundary. Neumann’s extended Bloch-group construction also records logarithmic choices needed for the complex Cheeger–Chern–Simons invariant. A second route: circle patterns for general augmented plats

For a connected labeled right-angled augmented plat at any fixed bridge width, the signed complete-source theorem identifies the original source rate with the actual complete volume. The fixed complex core theorem now gives a nonzero analytic asymptotic for its complete-image external-color term. This construction is independent of the strict-tree recursion; the statements below retain their own hypotheses.

A circle pattern represents the labeled boundary faces by circles or lines at infinity, with the prescribed tangencies. The marked circle-pattern theorem reconstructs the two exceptional boundary faces as parallel lines and solves simultaneously for all finite circle centers and radii. The original positive face Hessian, followed by elementary rigidity of the center triangles, proves that this system has an invertible Jacobian. Its unique nearby solution has exactly the stationary radii of the source action, with one shared strip width and the selected boundary eigenlines retained.

The primitive collar theorem computes the actual side pairing, selected eigenflag and half-twist shear, including a common marked-line limit. The global geometric assembly verifies the full original group presentation and identifies the core’s limiting action with the marked geometric deformation. For complex meridian logs it retains the boundary correction 12∑iRe⁡UiIm⁡Vi\tfrac12\sum_i\operatorname{Re}U_i\operatorname{Im}V_i; for real external deficits this correction vanishes. For an unrestricted plat, these geometric and local core statements still need bounds on the remaining reflected-color terms before they imply a full filling theorem. Several separately certified plat families and the strict-tree class already have those bounds; see the results and remaining scope.

An identity smoothing replaces a specified crossing-disk coupon by the identity and retains the remaining face identifications. For such a diagram, the block capacity theorem removes the need to assume an attained positive-radius minimum. The chain argument uses a controlled finite degeneration: cut corners have a common horospherical height and mutual distance O(T−1)O(T^{-1}). The global angle slice carries the topology

An actual ideal triangulation specifies which faces are glued, which tetrahedron edges belong to each manifold edge, and which peripheral curves represent meridians and longitudes. Its angle constraints must retain this incidence.

At a candidate maximum, stationarity must hold in every allowed angle direction. The corresponding shape and length equations must make adjacent cells agree. The resulting developing map must have the intended winding and end behavior. Complete cusps, or the specified filled peripheral equations, are part of the certificate.

The source-volume certificate keeps these requirements explicit. An algebraic critical point can be isolated and still belong to the wrong component or have the wrong peripheral marking.

A general certificate through volume maximization

For an ideal tetrahedron, its three independent positive dihedral angles α,β,γ\alpha,\beta,\gamma have sum π\pi, and its volume is Λ(α)+Λ(β)+Λ(γ),Λ(t)=−∫0tlog⁡|2sin⁡u|du. \Lambda(\alpha)+\Lambda(\beta)+\Lambda(\gamma),\qquad \Lambda(t)=-\int_0^t\log|2\sin u|\,du. An ideal triangulation imposes additional linear sums 2π2\pi around the manifold edges. These equations define its angle polytope. Casson and Rivin’s variational method recovers the nonlinear shape equations by differentiating the sum of these volumes. Rivin’s 1994 paper is a primary source for the volume method; the proof by Futer and Guéritaud explains the completeness implication in terms of angle variations.

For an actual marked ideal triangulation with torus vertex links, the positive gluing criterion gives a direct proof of the Casson–Rivin equivalence: an interior critical point of the ideal-tetrahedron volume function is exactly a complete positive gluing. Strict concavity makes it the unique maximum, including over the closed angle polytope, and its value is the actual complement volume.

The proof keeps the geometry explicit. Lagrange multipliers prescribe one truncated length for each global edge. These lengths determine compatible corner horospheres; the face maps then match them, giving complete flat cusp tori. Conversely, compatible horospheres make the geometric volume stationary under all allowed angle variations.

There is a finite algebraic certificate using positive upper-half-plane phases and positive multiplier variables. The unique optimum has algebraic shapes. Neither its dilogarithm volume nor the original quantum period is asserted to be an algebraic number. Existence of an interior optimum on a proposed triangulation, and selection of its period by the quantum source, remain distinct questions.

For fixed critical shapes, choosing a horosphere at one cusp and then rescaling it changes the decoration but leaves the hyperbolic metric unchanged. With cc cusps these independent choices form a torsor for (ℝ>0)c(\mathbb R_{>0})^c: there is one and only one tuple of scales taking one decoration to another. This accounts for the decoration freedom in the algebraic certificate. It does not yet determine the quantum period, and extending the action to complex scalars requires retaining its sign kernels.

For augmented plats, the actual matching-graph construction is one way to supply this certificate: a positive labeled right-angled polyhedron gives the marked complete exterior and an explicit positive ideal triangulation. The general gluing criterion does not require that particular polyhedral decomposition or a bound on bridge width.
