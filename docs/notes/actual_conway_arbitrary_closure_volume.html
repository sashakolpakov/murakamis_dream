Volume limits after reducing a Conway tangle closure

31 October 2026.

The earlier net-twist theorem starts with a cylindrical closure of a product. Here the input may have either of the two usual closures, may begin with a turn, and may contain twist sums that vanish exactly. Cutting along the resulting two-punctured spheres decomposes the knot into factors. When the remaining non-elementary factors have sufficiently large net twists, a hyperbolic input must be isotopic to just one of them.

This gives a broader criterion for recognizing covered presentations. Every knot it admits is explicitly isotopic to a long strict knot from the earlier theorem. The argument does not establish a new estimate for short twists or enlarge that previously covered set of knot types.

The proof has three distinct inputs. First, actual ordered tangle moves give a componentwise connected-sum decomposition. Second, the established strict-presentation theorem identifies each sufficiently long strict knot factor as hyperbolic and proves its volume limit. Third, an elementary group argument shows that a hyperbolic input knot cannot have two nontrivial factors of these known types. Consequently it is isotopic to one long strict factor. No quantum connected-sum formula is needed.

1. The fixed construction and the conclusion

A two-string tangle consists of two arcs with four prescribed boundary endpoints in a ball; closed components, if a composition creates them, must also be retained. The endpoints have the original cyclic order. Let RnR^n be the integral two-braid with signed crossing number nn, let TT rotate the ball through a quarter-turn, and put hi(ni)=T(Rni)h_i(n_i)=T(R^{n_i}). Form a finite expression EE from the labelled pieces hih_i, quarter-turns, and ordered vertical stacking. Each label occurs once. Allow either cylindrical closure C(E)C(E), joining the upper endpoints to their corresponding lower endpoints, or cap closure D(E)=C(TE)D(E)=C(TE), joining each upper pair and each lower pair.

Fix the expression, with m≥1m\ge1 labelled pieces, and its closing operation before choosing the integer tuple 𝒏=(n1,…,nm)\mathbf n=(n_1,\ldots,n_m). For a nonempty label set BB, write

WB(𝒏)=∑i∈Bni. W_B(\mathbf n)=\sum_{i\in B}n_i. \tag{1}

Apply the deterministic topological reduction described in Section 2. It returns a tree of componentwise connected sums of:

  1. strict literal cylindrical product presentations, with nonzero net weights attached to disjoint label blocks;
  2. integral braid closures C(Rs)C(R^s);
  3. unknots D(Rs)D(R^s).

Numerical-zero blocks removed during the reduction are also recorded. Thus surviving and deleted blocks partition the original labels. The strict factors use the finite L,H,AL,H,A test of the existing theorem. Each basic piece starts with label LL. The labels record intervals of possible normalized output states in the angle calculation: L=(0,1/2)L=(0,1/2), H=(1/2,1)H=(1/2,1), and A=(0,1)A=(0,1). They are not twist numbers. A turn TT exchanges L,HL,H and fixes AA; products LL,LA,AALL,LA,AA have type AA, products HL,HAHL,HA have type HH, and HHHH is forbidden. A closing product must have types AA,AL,AH,LHAA,AL,AH,LH, in either order.

Theorem. There is a finite number BEB_E, depending only on the fixed expression and the choice CC or DD, with the following property. Fix any integer tuple 𝒏\mathbf n. Suppose:

  • the literal filled input is a hyperbolic knot KK;
  • every net weight on every surviving strict factor of the specified reduction satisfies |WB(𝒏)|>BE|W_B(\mathbf n)|>B_E.

Then the reduction has exactly one strict factor. Every other terminal factor is an unknot, and KK is ambient isotopic to that strict factor. In particular,

limN→∞2πNlog⁡|KN(K)|=Vol⁡(S3\K). \lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{2\pi}{N}\log|K_N(K)| =\operatorname{Vol}(S^3\setminus K). \tag{2}

The invariant is the existing reduced enhanced Kashaev invariant with unknot value one. The limit includes both parities and composite levels. All weights, their zero relations, and the knot are fixed before NN tends to infinity. Their signs and relative sizes are unrestricted. No lower bound is imposed on weights carried only by elementary terminal factors. The hypothesis on the strict factors is not a claim that short surviving strict weights can be ignored.

2. The actual reductions and their termination

The required topological algorithm is the optional reduction in the ordered net-twist theorem, Appendices A and B. Its essential moves are recalled to make clear which topology, rather than scalar source algebra, is used.

Write τ=T2\tau=T^2 for the actual planar half-turn. It reverses the order of a stack and is retained on subexpressions. Rotation about the stacking axis is denoted ρ\rho. The empty-collar isotopy and the original closed pairing give

RaF≃ρa(F)Ra,RaRb≃Ra+b,C((TF)G)≃C(F(TG)). R^aF\simeq \rho^a(F)R^a,\qquad R^aR^b\simeq R^{a+b},\qquad C((TF)G)\simeq C(F(TG)). \tag{3}

The first move rotates the intervening tangle; it does not assert that the tangle is invariant under that rotation. The rotation depends only on the parity of aa, preserves crossing signs, and is pushed through the ordered syntax by the actual rotation identities. The closed pairing in the last move has its recorded reflection and no extra boundary twist. Cyclically moving the cut in the closed cylinder also gives C(FG)≃C(GF)C(FG)\simeq C(GF).

To expose a selected leaf, follow its path to the root. Associativity and closed cyclicity transfer each sibling into the other factor; the last identity of (3) transfers every encountered turn. This terminates at a form C(hiQ)C(h_iQ), retaining every other leaf exactly once and recording every induced order. If the selected weight is zero,

C(hi(0)Q)≃D(Q). C(h_i(0)Q)\simeq D(Q). \tag{4}

This uses hi(0)=T(R0)h_i(0)=T(R^0), the cup-cap tangle; it is not the through-identity tangle.

At a cap-closed root one has

D(TF)=C(τF),D(FG)=D(F)#D(G). D(TF)=C(\tau F),\qquad D(FG)=D(F)\mathbin{\#}D(G). \tag{5}

In the second operation, the upper-cap component of D(F)D(F) is joined to the lower-cap component of D(G)D(G). The horizontal empty collar contains a sphere meeting the link in exactly two points. Cutting it and capping its punctures gives the two stated cap closures. Hence this is an exhibited componentwise connected sum, with its components and marks specified. It is not a four-punctured Conway mutation.

The diagram uses bottom-to-top stacking: FF is below GG. The amber arcs are the new upper cap of FF and lower cap of GG. Joining along these arcs reconstructs the original stack. This specifies the connected sum even when a cap closure has more than one component.

For a cylindrical product, the established ordered normalization gathers direct integral factors using (3), fuses them, and records their disjoint label unions. Any resulting zero weight can be removed by (4). With no remaining zeros, its output is strict or is one integral braid closure. The possible failed LLLL closing pair is resolved by

C((TU)(TV))≃C(U(τV)), C((TU)(TV))\simeq C(U(\tau V)), \tag{6}

which exposes two integral factors for a genuine fusion. Root turns switch CC and DD, retaining τ\tau where (5) requires it. A single box terminates in one of

C(Rs),D(Rs),C(h(s))=D(Rs),D(h(s))=C(Rs). C(R^s),\quad D(R^s),\quad C(h(s))=D(R^s),\quad D(h(s))=C(R^s). \tag{7}

Here C(Rs)C(R^s) is the (2,s)(2,s) torus braid closure and D(Rs)D(R^s) is an unknot. The latter isotopy moves successive crossings into the end cap and removes curls. Any ribbon framing is transported and recorded, not silently replaced by a blackboard convention. At s=0s=0, C(R0)C(R^0) is the two-component unlink.

The one-box rule is applied before a numerical-zero deletion. In particular a last zero box terminates as the appropriate unknot or two-component unlink in (7); it is not removed to create an unspecified empty scalar or an unrecorded component.

Fix a rule for selecting the next operation; for example, give the one-box rule priority, process the root closure next, and choose the first eligible leaf or stack in the written order for the remaining steps. Use this same rule for every integer assignment. Every fusion or zero deletion lowers the number of live labelled blocks. Between such events, shortening turn chains, flattening stacks, and processing cap roots removes vertices from the finite expression forest. Exposing a chosen zero follows a finite path without copying its siblings. Thus the process terminates. Connected-sum edges form a tree because each arises by splitting one pending factor into two; rotations and later reductions act within these factors and retain the attaching arcs.

3. Finitely many output types and one threshold

The algorithm only tests two kinds of integer data:

(nimod⁡2)i=1m,(𝟏WB(𝒏)=0)⌀≠B⊂{1,…,m}. \bigl(n_i\bmod2\bigr)_{i=1}^m,\qquad \bigl(\mathbf 1_{W_B(\mathbf n)=0}\bigr)_{ \varnothing\ne B\subset\{1,\ldots,m\}}. \tag{8}

The first determines every ρWB\rho^{W_B} decoration. The second determines every numerical-zero deletion. Rotations preserve the signs of the integral crossings, fusion takes an ordinary sum, and no step copies a label. Every integer expression tested is consequently one of the subset sums (1), not an unbounded new family of linear forms.

There are finitely many patterns (8), although some are not realized by integer tuples. On each realized pattern, the chosen schedule, ordered output templates, attaching components, and surviving/deleted label blocks are fixed. Their integer weights remain the displayed linear forms. In particular, approaching another zero wall may change the partition, but there are only finitely many such possibilities.

Let 𝒮E\mathcal S_E be the finite set of strict terminal templates that occur among the realized patterns. For each template σ\sigma, the existing strict-tree theorem supplies a finite threshold B(σ)B(\sigma), uniform over its independently signed weights and its knot-producing parity choices. Set

BE=max⁡({2}∪{B(σ):σ∈𝒮E}). B_E=\max\!\left(\{2\}\cup \{B(\sigma):\sigma\in\mathcal S_E\}\right). \tag{9}

This maximum is finite. It gives a single threshold for the fixed input, over all input parities and all subset-sum zero patterns. It is an existence and finite-reduction statement; no effective numerical algorithm for the analytic thresholds is added here.

4. A knot input makes every terminal factor a knot

Retain all closed components throughout the reductions. If cv≥1c_v\ge1 is the number of components of terminal factor vv, and the decomposition tree has rr vertices, componentwise connected sum gives

c(K)=∑vcv−(r−1)=1+∑v(cv−1). c(K)=\sum_v c_v-(r-1) =1+\sum_v(c_v-1). \tag{10}

Thus a one-component input forces cv=1c_v=1 for every factor. In particular, no even-ss torus braid closure or two-component zero closure can occur among the terminal factors of the knot.

Within a terminal tangle expression, a circle already closed in a subtree cannot reconnect to an outside endpoint. The remaining two arcs still close to at least one other component. Therefore a terminal knot has no such internal circle. Its whole component test, including these circles, satisfies the hypothesis of the strict-tree theorem.

By (9) and the assumed longness, every strict terminal factor is a hyperbolic knot with the established volume limit. A terminal elementary knot is either an unknot or a (2,s)(2,s) torus knot with odd |s|≥3|s|\ge3. No assertion is being made about the primeness of an unknown short strict factor: the longness hypothesis has removed that case.

5. The group obstruction to two nontrivial factors

Let μ\mu denote a knot meridian. Its class generates the infinite cyclic abelianization, so its cyclic subgroup is infinite and injective. Van Kampen applied to the meridional annulus of a connected sum gives

π1(XK1#K2)≅π1(XK1)*⟨μ⟩π1(XK2), \pi_1(X_{K_1\#K_2}) \cong \pi_1(X_{K_1})*_{\langle\mu\rangle}\pi_1(X_{K_2}), \tag{11}

where the two meridians are identified, with their orientation carried through the attaching arcs. Iterating over the exhibited sum tree gives the analogous amalgam of all factor groups over the same infinite cyclic subgroup. The usual reduced-word proof for an amalgam injects each factor group. If uu and vv belong to different factor groups and neither belongs to ⟨μ⟩\langle\mu\rangle, the word

uvu−1v−1 uvu^{-1}v^{-1} \tag{12}

is a reduced word of length four, and is not the identity.

Each nontrivial factor in Section 4 has an element outside ⟨μ⟩\langle\mu\rangle which commutes with μ\mu.

For a long hyperbolic factor, take its preferred longitude. Its complete rank-two cusp injects the peripheral ℤ2\mathbb Z^2 into the group, so the longitude is not a power of the meridian.

For a (2,s)(2,s) torus knot, odd |s|≥3|s|\ge3, use its central fiber. Here is an argument independent of a longitude sign convention. The two solid tori on either side of the standard torus, joined through the annulus left by the knot neighborhood, give

Gs=⟨a,b∣a2=bs⟩,z=a2=bs. G_s=\langle a,b\mid a^2=b^s\rangle,\qquad z=a^2=b^s. \tag{13}

The annulus winds twice around one core and ss times around the other, which gives this relation. The element zz is central. Abelianization is infinite cyclic, with [a]=st[a]=s t, [b]=2t[b]=2t, and [z]=2st[z]=2s t. A meridian has class ±t\pm t.

The quotient by ⟨z⟩\langle z\rangle is C2*C|s|C_2*C_{|s|}. Its abelianization is cyclic of order 2|s|2|s|, and the image of any meridian is a generator of that abelianization. A finite-order element in a free product is conjugate into a factor: after cyclic reduction, a word of length at least two has powers of strictly increasing reduced length. Its finite order would therefore divide 22 or |s||s|, incompatible with abelianization order 2|s|2|s|. The image of μ\mu in this quotient has infinite order.

It follows that z∉⟨μ⟩z\notin\langle\mu\rangle. Indeed z=μkz=\mu^k would force k=±2sk=\pm2s in abelianization and then force a nonzero power of the infinite-order image of μ\mu to vanish in the quotient. More strongly, a relation μuzv=1\mu^u z^v=1 first gives u=0u=0 in the quotient and then v=0v=0 by abelianization. Thus ⟨μ,z⟩≅ℤ2\langle\mu,z\rangle\cong\mathbb Z^2. This proves the precise commuting-element fact needed below without assuming a peripheral injection theorem for torus knots. In conventional oriented torus coordinates the preferred longitude is zμ−2sz\mu^{-2s}, because the torus push-off has linking number 2s2s; that additional identification is not used.

Now suppose two terminal factors are nontrivial. Choose the elements just constructed in their two groups. They both commute with the common meridian, while (12) proves that they do not commute with each other. Hence the centralizer of μ\mu in the input knot group is nonabelian.

This is impossible for the hyperbolic input. Its faithful complete holonomy sends a meridian to a nontrivial parabolic. Conjugate this parabolic to z↦z+1z\mapsto z+1. A Möbius transformation commuting with it must fix its unique point at infinity and have the form z↦z+cz\mapsto z+c. Such transformations commute. Faithfulness therefore makes the meridian centralizer in the knot group abelian.

There is at most one nontrivial terminal factor.

6. Selecting the strict factor and transporting the conclusion

If every factor were an unknot, the input would be an unknot. If its only nontrivial factor were a torus knot, the input would be that torus knot. The first has cyclic group. The second has the nontrivial central element zz of (13), so neither can be a finite-volume hyperbolic knot. For completeness, the center of such a hyperbolic knot group is trivial: a central element commutes with the peripheral parabolic group and is itself a translation fixing that cusp point; centrality would force every group element to commute with this nontrivial translation. The same Möbius calculation then makes every element a translation. A discrete translation group has an infinite-volume quotient of all of hyperbolic three-space, even when its quotient horospheres are compact. It cannot be the complete finite-volume knot group.

Consequently the unique nontrivial factor is strict, and all other terminal factors are unknots. Connected sum with each exhibited unknot is removed by straightening the trivial summing arc across its spanning disk. This gives an actual orientation-preserving ambient isotopy from the input knot to the one strict terminal knot K*K_*.

Restricting that isotopy to transported tubular neighborhoods gives a homeomorphism of exteriors carrying the meridian and preferred zero-linking longitude. Pull back the already constructed complete hyperbolic metric of S3\K*S^3\setminus K_*; this supplies the same complete metric and volume on the input complement. There is no attempt to identify the different augmented exteriors before auxiliary filling.

The reduced enhanced Kashaev invariant is the invariant of this same zero-framed knot, so KN(K)=KN(K*)K_N(K)=K_N(K_*) at every level. If ribbon framings are kept during the moves, their correction is the existing unit complete-color twist; zero-framing removes it, and its modulus would not change the logarithmic rate in any event. The full one-cut modified value retains its single factor

𝒩N(K;1)=iN+3NKN(K). \mathcal N_N(K;1)=\frac{i^{N+3}}{N}K_N(K). \tag{14}

Apply the inherited strict-tree volume theorem to K*K_*. These exact identifications prove (2). Neither multiplicativity of KNK_N nor additivity of quantum exponential rates over a connected sum is used.

7. Scope of the extension

The conclusion applies to either closing operation on every fixed finite expression made by the stated actual Conway operations, including initial root turns and all numerical-zero walls. The prescribed reduction and the uniform threshold account for all their finitely many parity and zero patterns.

The hypothesis that the original filled link is a hyperbolic knot is essential to this argument. For a composite input, two long strict factors can remain, and (12) correctly detects the obstruction. No new quantum connected-sum theorem has been proved here.

Long written twists alone do not imply the hypothesis on surviving strict weights: opposite large twists can cancel. Conversely, original weights or intermediate sums may be zero; only final weights in strict terminal factors must satisfy (9). The theorem does not give a short-twist assertion or a threshold uniform over growing expressions.

The topological input is the actual ordered normalization and its two-punctured cap spheres, not the commutativity of the colored source. The analytic and geometric long-filling theorem is inherited for the strict terminal presentation. The new step is the finite all-root reduction together with the group argument forcing a hyperbolic input to equal one of those known strict knots.

8. A finite topological criterion and simple conditions on the integers

The reduction itself requires no hyperbolic metric. It uses the ordered tangle expression, its endpoint connections, and the integer sums in (1). Under the longness assumption of the theorem, its output also determines whether a knot input is hyperbolic:

Corollary 1. Suppose the input has one component and every surviving strict-factor weight has magnitude greater than BEB_E. The input is hyperbolic if and only if its reduction has exactly one strict factor and every other terminal factor is an unknot.

Indeed, the forward implication is Sections 4–6. For the reverse implication, straighten the summing arcs of the unknot factors. The resulting knot is the long strict factor, whose complete hyperbolic metric is supplied by the strict-tree theorem.

The elementary factors are explicit. A factor D(Rs)D(R^s) is always an unknot. A factor C(Rs)C(R^s) is a knot only for odd ss, and among those knots it is an unknot exactly when s=1s=1 or s=−1s=-1. Consequently the reduction gives a finite topological test once the longness hypotheses have been established. Determining whether an elementary factor is trivial requires these tests for s=±1s=\pm1, in addition to the parity and zero tests used to choose the output templates. No numerical values for the inherited analytic thresholds are supplied by this argument.

There is also a sufficient condition stated entirely in the original integer parameters. It is stronger than the condition on surviving strict factors, but avoids computing which of their sums will survive.

Corollary 2. Fix the expression and closure, and suppose that

∑i∈Bni=0or|∑i∈Bni|>BEfor every nonempty B⊆{1,…,m}. \sum_{i\in B}n_i=0 \quad\text{or}\quad \left|\sum_{i\in B}n_i\right|>B_E \qquad\text{for every nonempty }B\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}. \tag{15}

If the resulting knot is hyperbolic, it satisfies (2). In particular, fix any integer vector 𝒂\mathbf a and set 𝒏=t𝒂\mathbf n=t\mathbf a. For every integer tt with |t|>BE|t|>B_E, every hyperbolic knot obtained in this way satisfies the volume equality. The parameter tt is fixed before the quantum level NN tends to infinity.

To prove the corollary, each surviving strict weight is a nonzero subset sum, so (15) implies the theorem’s hypothesis. On the integer ray, such a sum is t∑i∈Bait\sum_{i\in B}a_i; its magnitude is at least |t||t| unless it vanishes. This also allows zero entries and exact cancellations in 𝒂\mathbf a. The corollary does not say that every ray contains hyperbolic knots; Corollary 1 determines the topological alternative under the same longness condition. Another sufficient special case of (15) is that all original integers have the same sign and each has magnitude greater than BEB_E.

9. The operator period and the volume of this complement

The pointed quantum AA-period theorem applies to the same reduced knot invariant. Fix a genuine annihilator PP, its prescribed finite vector of initial ordinary-color polynomials c0(q)c_0(q), and the companion row rN(q)r_N(q). With qN=eπi/Nq_N=e^{\pi i/N}, its exact reconstruction is

𝒫N(P,c0)=CT⁡u=0[rN(qN(1+u))c0(qN(1+u))]=KN(K). \mathscr P_N(P,c_0) =\operatorname{CT}_{u=0} \bigl[r_N(q_N(1+u))c_0(q_N(1+u))\bigr] =K_N(K). \tag{16}

The product of Laurent germs is formed before its constant term is taken. This retains the derivatives needed at singular recurrence steps. The required number of initial derivatives depends on the fixed operator, not on NN. For every knot satisfying the theorem,

limN→∞2πNlog⁡|𝒫N(P,c0)|=Vol⁡(S3\K). \lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{2\pi}{N}\log|\mathscr P_N(P,c_0)| =\operatorname{Vol}(S^3\setminus K). \tag{17}

The positive Gram determinant ratio of the same pointing theorem has the exact value 𝒢N=∑j=j0N|Cj(qN)|2\mathscr G_N=\sum_{j=j_0}^{N}|C_j(q_N)|^2. Its ordinary-color terms have a polynomial total bound. Therefore (π/N)log⁡𝒢N(\pi/N)\log\mathscr G_N tends to this same positive volume. These conclusions transfer through the actual knot isotopy of Section 6; they do not compare unrelated recurrence operators or their classical polynomial factors. The classical geometric-factor question remains separate.
