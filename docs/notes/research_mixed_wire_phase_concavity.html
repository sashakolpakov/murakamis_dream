Concavity of the mixed Newton wire and filling phase

Research proof, 3 November 2026. Frozen manuscripts are unchanged.

The phase obtained by retaining one Newton index has a useful concavity region that does not depend on the filling power. It applies before the shared wire is summed out. In particular, it does not require the full bare kernel to have a geometric asymptotic at a large complex angle.

The theorem below concerns the explicit holomorphic phase. It gives a strict maximum and quantitative boundary gaps on a specified convex contour domain. An application to an exact finite source must still justify its local interpolation and control the rest of its integration cycle.

1. The phase and its regular factorial domain

Use the original sine-factorial primitive JJ, normalized by J′(z)=Log⁡(2sin⁡πz),J(0)=0.(1) J'(z)=\operatorname{Log}(2\sin\pi z),\qquad J(0)=0. \qquad\text{(1)} Its branch is holomorphic on 0<ℜz<10<\Re z<1 and agrees with the real logarithm on 0<z<10<z<1. The original Newton phase and the complete-end edge phase satisfy b(x,t)=−J(1/2−x+t/(2π))−J(1/2−x−t/(2π))−2J(x),ψ(t,0)=−4J(1/4+t/(4π))−4J(1/4−t/(4π)).(2) \begin{split} b(x,t)&=-J(1/2-x+t/(2\pi)) -J(1/2-x-t/(2\pi))-2J(x),\\ \psi(t,0)&=-4J(1/4+t/(4\pi)) -4J(1/4-t/(4\pi)). \end{split} \qquad\text{(2)} The second equality has the original normalization ψ(0,0)=M∞=4G/π\psi(0,0)=M_\infty=4G/\pi. Differentiating it gives ψt(t,0)=−π−1arsinh⁡(tan⁡(t/2))\psi_t(t,0)=-\pi^{-1}\operatorname{arsinh}(\tan(t/2)) on the branch through zero.

For a real power nn, retain the mixed phase ℱn(x,t)=b(x,t)+ψ(t,0)+int24π−it.(3) \mathcal F_n(x,t) =b(x,t)+\psi(t,0)+\frac{int^2}{4\pi}-it. \qquad\text{(3)} Here xx is the Newton index divided by the level, and tt is the original angular variable at the selected vertex. The last two terms are the original crossing and unit-charge terms.

Put s=12−t2π,x=a+iY,s=b+iS,0≤Y≤S.(4) s=\frac12-\frac{t}{2\pi},\qquad x=a+iY,\quad s=b+iS,\qquad 0\le Y\le S. \qquad\text{(4)} The letter bb without arguments in (4) denotes ℜs\Re s; the Newton phase is always written b(x,t)b(x,t). For 0<a<b<1/20<a<b<1/2, all three Newton factorial arguments x,s−x,1−s−xx,s-x,1-s-x have real parts strictly between zero and one. The same holds for the arguments of the complete-end phase in (2).

Reflection and duplication of the sine primitive give J(1−z)=−J(z),J(z)=2J(z/2)+2J(1/2+z/2).(5) J(1-z)=-J(z),\qquad J(z)=2J(z/2)+2J(1/2+z/2). \qquad\text{(5)} Both identities follow by differentiating and fixing the constant on the real interval. Analytic continuation in their regular strips retains these constants. Consequently the part of (3) preceding the filling terms is ℋ(x,s)=J(s+x)−J(s−x)−2J(x)+2J(s)−8J(s/2).(6) \mathcal H(x,s) =J(s+x)-J(s-x)-2J(x)+2J(s)-8J(s/2). \qquad\text{(6)}

2. A convex domain with strictly negative real Hessian

For h≥0h\ge0, define α(h)=12πarccos⁡(sech⁡(2πh)).(7) \alpha(h)=\frac1{2\pi} \arccos(\operatorname{sech}(2\pi h)). \qquad\text{(7)} Thus 0≤α(h)<1/40\le\alpha(h)<1/4. At fixed heights Y,SY,S, let 𝒫Y,S={(a,b):α(Y)<a<b<1/2,α(S)<b}.(8) \mathcal P_{Y,S} =\{(a,b):\alpha(Y)<a<b<1/2,\ \alpha(S)<b\}. \qquad\text{(8)} This is a convex open polygon. Its inequalities are independent of the real power nn.

Theorem 1. For every 0≤Y≤S0\le Y\le S and every real nn, the function (a,b)↦ℜℱn(a+iY,π−2π(b+iS))(9) (a,b)\longmapsto \Re\mathcal F_n(a+iY,\ \pi-2\pi(b+iS)) \qquad\text{(9)} has strictly negative Hessian on 𝒫Y,S\mathcal P_{Y,S}.

Proof. Define four real quantities, with the indicated complex arguments retained: u=ℜcot⁡π(s+x),v=ℜcot⁡π(s−x),w=ℜcot⁡πx,d=ℜcsc⁡πs.(10) \begin{split} u&=\Re\cot\pi(s+x),& v&=\Re\cot\pi(s-x),\\ w&=\Re\cot\pi x,& d&=\Re\csc\pi s. \end{split} \qquad\text{(10)} Differentiating (6) twice, and using cot⁡(z/2)−cot⁡z=csc⁡z\cot(z/2)-\cot z=\csc z, gives the negative real Hessian 𝖧=π(v+2w−u−v−u−v−uv+2d−u).(11) \mathsf H =\pi \begin{pmatrix} v+2w-u&-v-u\\ -v-u&v+2d-u \end{pmatrix}. \qquad\text{(11)} The filling terms in (3) have affine real parts on a contour of fixed Y,SY,S, so they make no contribution to this matrix.

The inequalities 0<a<b<1/20<a<b<1/2 imply v,w,d>0v,w,d>0. If a+b≥1/2a+b\ge1/2, then u≤0u\le0, and (11) is the sum of the positive diagonal matrix 2πdiag⁡(w,d)2\pi\operatorname{diag}(w,d) and two positive semidefinite rank-one matrices.

Suppose instead that a+b<1/2a+b<1/2, so u>0u>0. For 0<r<1/20<r<1/2 and h≥0h\ge0, write C(r,h)=ℜcot⁡π(r+ih)=sin⁡(2πr)cosh⁡(2πh)−cos⁡(2πr).(12) C(r,h)=\Re\cot\pi(r+ih) =\frac{\sin(2\pi r)} {\cosh(2\pi h)-\cos(2\pi r)}. \qquad\text{(12)} It decreases as hh increases. Its derivative in rr is Cr(r,h)=2π{cos⁡(2πr)cosh⁡(2πh)−1}{cosh⁡(2πh)−cos⁡(2πr)}2.(13) C_r(r,h)= \frac{2\pi\{\cos(2\pi r)\cosh(2\pi h)-1\}} {\{\cosh(2\pi h)-\cos(2\pi r)\}^2}. \qquad\text{(13)} In particular Cr(r,h)<0C_r(r,h)<0 when r>α(h)r>\alpha(h). Once this condition holds, it continues to hold as rr increases within (0,1/2)(0,1/2).

Starting at (a,Y)(a,Y), first increase the real argument to a+ba+b, and then increase the height to Y+SY+S. Equations (8), (12), and (13) give u<wu<w. Starting instead at (b,S)(b,S) gives u<ℜcot⁡πsu<\Re\cot\pi s. Finally d−ℜcot⁡πs=ℜtan⁡(πs/2)>0,(14) d-\Re\cot\pi s=\Re\tan(\pi s/2)>0, \qquad\text{(14)} so u<du<d. The strict last inequality follows from 0<ℜs<1/20<\Re s<1/2.

Now (11) becomes 𝖧=π(v+u)(1−1−11)+2πdiag⁡(w−u,d−u),(15) \mathsf H =\pi(v+u) \begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix} +2\pi\operatorname{diag}(w-u,d-u), \qquad\text{(15)} which is positive definite. This proves the theorem. ▫\square

3. An explicit coercive bound on compact domains

Put u+=max⁡(u,0)u_+=\max(u,0). The proof gives the pointwise matrix bound 𝖧≽2πmin⁡{w−u+,d−u+}I.(16) \mathsf H\ \succeq 2\pi\min\{w-u_+,d-u_+\}\,I. \qquad\text{(16)} Both entries inside the minimum are strictly positive on (8). Let QQ be a compact convex subset of that polygon, and set μQ=infQmin⁡{w−u+,d−u+}>0.(17) \mu_Q=\inf_Q\min\{w-u_+,d-u_+\}>0. \qquad\text{(17)} This constant is defined by elementary trigonometric functions on a compact set. Any explicit lower bound for it can be substituted in the following estimate.

Corollary 2. Suppose the holomorphic phase (3) has a critical point (x*,t*)(x_*,t_*) on the fixed-height contour over QQ. Write x*=a*+iYx_*=a_*+iY and s*=1/2−t*/(2π)=b*+iSs_*=1/2-t_*/(2\pi)=b_*+iS. Then for every (a,b)∈Q(a,b)\in Q, ℜℱn(a+iY,π−2π(b+iS))≤ℜℱn(x*,t*)−πμQ((a−a*)2+(b−b*)2).(18) \Re\mathcal F_n(a+iY,\pi-2\pi(b+iS)) \le \Re\mathcal F_n(x_*,t_*) -\pi\mu_Q\bigl((a-a_*)^2+(b-b_*)^2\bigr). \qquad\text{(18)} In particular the critical point is the unique real maximum on this contour domain. A boundary set at Euclidean distance at least δ\delta from (a*,b*)(a_*,b_*) loses at least πμQδ2\pi\mu_Q\delta^2 in its exponential rate.

Proof. The real gradient vanishes at the holomorphic critical point. Integrate (16) along the segment from it to (a,b)(a,b), which lies in QQ by convexity. ▫\square

The ordinary angular distance satisfies b−b*=−(ℜt−ℜt*)/(2π)b-b_*=-(\Re t-\Re t_*)/(2\pi); this supplies the precise conversion from (18) to angular coordinates.

4. Two selected vertices and their shared wire

For powers n,pn,p, the mixed phase of the internal pair is 𝒜n,p(x,t,r)=ℱn(x,t)+ℱp(x,r).(19) \mathcal A_{n,p}(x,t,r)= \mathcal F_n(x,t)+\mathcal F_p(x,r). \qquad\text{(19)} Thus the one shared Newton index is retained in both terms. Fix heights Y,S1,S2Y,S_1,S_2 with 0≤Y≤min⁡(S1,S2)0\le Y\le\min(S_1,S_2), and write x=a+iY,12−t2π=b1+iS1,12−r2π=b2+iS2.(20) x=a+iY,\qquad \frac12-\frac{t}{2\pi}=b_1+iS_1,\qquad \frac12-\frac{r}{2\pi}=b_2+iS_2. \qquad\text{(20)} The common real domain is the convex polyhedron α(Y)<a<bi<1/2,α(Si)<bi(i=1,2).(21) \alpha(Y)<a<b_i<1/2,\qquad \alpha(S_i)<b_i\quad(i=1,2). \qquad\text{(21)}

Corollary 3. The real part of (19) has strictly negative Hessian on (21), for every real n,pn,p. On a compact convex subset QQ, let μi>0\mu_i>0 be the infimum in (17) for its projection to (a,bi)(a,b_i). If a holomorphic critical point lies on this fixed-height contour, then ℜ𝒜n,p≤ℜ𝒜n,p,*−π[(μ1+μ2)(a−a*)2+μ1(b1−b1,*)2+μ2(b2−b2,*)2].(22) \begin{split} \Re\mathcal A_{n,p} \le \Re\mathcal A_{n,p,*} -\pi\bigl[ (\mu_1+\mu_2)(a-a_*)^2 +\mu_1(b_1-b_{1,*})^2 +\mu_2(b_2-b_{2,*})^2\bigr]. \end{split} \qquad\text{(22)}

Proof. Add the two Hessian bounds (16), each pulled back to its own two coordinates. Their sum is strictly positive in all three real directions. Integrate along a segment in the convex domain as in Corollary 2. ▫\square

All factorial arguments remain in their regular strips on (21). This controls the central mixed phase and its real contour faces. It does not yet control indices outside that domain, the other Fourier periods, first-normal remainders, or the interpolation error for the actual coefficient functions. In particular, the exact Fourier identity 𝒫n,p,N=N∫Wn,N(x)Wp,N(x)dx\mathcal P_{n,p,N}=N\int W_{n,N}(x)W_{p,N}(x)\,dx still needs estimates on the complementary part of its full period before (22) can be used to prove its leading coefficient.
