A convexity criterion for the original edge phase

Research note, 2 November 2026. This note gives an exact test for the real part of the Hessian of the circle-tree edge phase. A simple sufficient condition can be checked without solving any filling equations. The test is unchanged by arbitrary signed filling powers.

The conclusion is a uniqueness and comparison theorem inside a specified convex complex region. It does not assert that every critical point of the original source lies in that region. In particular, it does not supply the global hypothesis in the dominant-saddle transport theorem.

1. The original phase and its branches

Use the edge phase of the circle-tree geometry paper, with its normalization

ψ(0,0)=voctπ,ψt(t,u)=−1πarsinh⁡(tan⁡(t/2)cos⁡(u/2)).(1) \psi(0,0)=\frac{v_{\mathrm{ oct}}}{\pi},\qquad \psi_t(t,u)=-\frac1\pi\operatorname{arsinh} \bigl(\tan(t/2)\cos(u/2)\bigr). \qquad\text{(1)}

The formula for ψu\psi_u is obtained by interchanging t,ut,u. Here voctv_{\mathrm{ oct}} is the volume of a regular ideal octahedron. The logarithms initially have their values at t=u=0t=u=0, and are then continued along the specified geometric path. When a larger domain is used, a single-valued lift of this phase is part of the data. The Hessian calculated below does not forget that choice; it is the continuation of the Hessian in (1).

Put

a=tan⁡(t/2),b=tan⁡(u/2),q=1+a2+b2,q(0,0)=1.(2) a=\tan(t/2),\qquad b=\tan(u/2),\qquad q=\sqrt{1+a^2+b^2},\qquad q(0,0)=1. \qquad\text{(2)}

We work where cos⁡(t/2)\cos(t/2), cos⁡(u/2)\cos(u/2), and qq are nonzero. In the test below the continued root is required to satisfy ℜq>0\Re q>0. This specifies a root, rather than just the equation for its square.

Differentiating (1) on its original branch gives

−2πψ″(t,u)=H(t,u)=1q(1+a2−ab−ab1+b2).(3) -2\pi\,\psi''(t,u) =H(t,u) =\frac1q \begin{pmatrix} 1+a^2&-ab\\ -ab&1+b^2 \end{pmatrix}. \qquad\text{(3)}

For example, the square root in the derivative of arsinh⁡(acos⁡(u/2))\operatorname{arsinh}(a\cos(u/2)) is qcos⁡(u/2)q\cos(u/2), by continuation from completeness. This gives the first diagonal entry in (3); differentiating with respect to uu gives the off-diagonal entry. Equivalently, with

Δ=qcos⁡(t/2)cos⁡(u/2), \Delta=q\cos(t/2)\cos(u/2),

the same matrix is

H=1Δ(cos⁡(u/2)/cos⁡(t/2)−sin⁡(t/2)sin⁡(u/2)−sin⁡(t/2)sin⁡(u/2)cos⁡(t/2)/cos⁡(u/2)).(4) H=\frac1\Delta \begin{pmatrix} \cos(u/2)/\cos(t/2)&-\sin(t/2)\sin(u/2)\\ -\sin(t/2)\sin(u/2)&\cos(t/2)/\cos(u/2) \end{pmatrix}. \qquad\text{(4)}

In particular,

det⁡H=1,tr⁡H=q+q−1.(5) \det H=1,\qquad \operatorname{tr}H=q+q^{-1}. \qquad\text{(5)}

All these are identities for the original edge phase. They do not use an approximation to it.

2. The exact positivity test

For a complex symmetric matrix HH, its Hermitian part is the real symmetric matrix obtained by taking the real part of each entry. We write this matrix as ℜH\Re H.

Proposition 1. Suppose that the branch in (2) has ℜq>0\Re q>0. Then

ℜH>0⇔ℜq>|ℑ(ab¯)|.(6) \Re H>0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \Re q>\bigl|\Im(a\overline b)\bigr|. \qquad\text{(6)}

Here >0>0 means positive definite. More explicitly,

det⁡(ℜH)=(ℜq)2−(ℑ(ab¯))2|q|2,tr⁡(ℜH)=ℜq(1+|q|−2).(7) \det(\Re H) =\frac{(\Re q)^2-\bigl(\Im(a\overline b)\bigr)^2}{|q|^2}, \qquad \operatorname{tr}(\Re H) =\Re q\,(1+|q|^{-2}). \qquad\text{(7)}

Proof. Write H=(ABBC)H=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A&B\\B&C\end{smallmatrix}\right). The identity AC−B2=1AC-B^2=1 gives

det⁡(ℜH)=1+ℜ(AC¯)−|B|22.(8) \det(\Re H) =\frac{1+\Re(A\overline C)-|B|^2}{2}. \qquad\text{(8)}

For (3), the difference in the numerator is

ℜ(AC¯)−|B|2=|q|−2[ℜ{(1+a2)(1+b¯2)}−|ab|2]=|q|−2[ℜ(q2)−2{ℑ(ab¯)}2]. \begin{aligned} \Re(A\overline C)-|B|^2 &=|q|^{-2} \left[\Re\{(1+a^2)(1+\overline b^{\,2})\}-|ab|^2\right]\\ &=|q|^{-2} \left[\Re(q^2)-2\{\Im(a\overline b)\}^2\right]. \end{aligned}

Since |q|2+ℜ(q2)=2(ℜq)2|q|^2+\Re(q^2)=2(\Re q)^2, substitution into (8) proves the determinant formula. The trace formula follows from (5). Its right-hand side is positive. A real symmetric two-by-two matrix with positive trace is positive definite exactly when its determinant is positive. This proves (6). ▫\square

The test is strict. At equality in (6), the real part of the Hessian has a zero eigenvalue, even though the complex determinant in (5) is still one.

3. An explicit sufficient region

Define

𝒞={(t,u):|ℜt|<π,|ℜu|<π,{ℑtan(t/2)}2+{ℑtan(u/2)}2<1}.(9) \mathcal C= \left\{(t,u): |\Re t|<\pi,\quad |\Re u|<\pi,\quad \{\Im\tan(t/2)\}^2+\{\Im\tan(u/2)\}^2<1 \right\}. \qquad\text{(9)}

On this region 1+a2+b21+a^2+b^2 has positive real part. Consequently its principal square root is holomorphic and has positive real part. It agrees with the complete branch wherever that branch is continued inside this region.

Corollary 2. The matrix ℜH(t,u)\Re H(t,u) is positive definite throughout 𝒞\mathcal C.

Proof. Write a=A+iCa=A+iC, b=B+iDb=B+iD, and set

X=A2+B2,Y=C2+D2<1,Z=AC+BD. X=A^2+B^2,\qquad Y=C^2+D^2<1,\qquad Z=AC+BD.

Then q2=1+X−Y+2iZq^2=1+X-Y+2iZ, so

(ℜq)2=|1+X−Y+2iZ|+1+X−Y2≥1+X−Y. (\Re q)^2 =\frac{|1+X-Y+2iZ|+1+X-Y}{2} \ge 1+X-Y.

Also {ℑ(ab¯)}2=(BC−AD)2≤XY\{\Im(a\overline b)\}^2=(BC-AD)^2\le XY, whereas

(1+X−Y)−XY=(1−Y)(1+X)>0. (1+X-Y)-XY=(1-Y)(1+X)>0.

Thus (6) holds strictly. ▫\square

Condition (9) is sufficient, not necessary. For example, take

t=2π3−2ilog⁡2,u=0.(10) t=\frac{2\pi}{3}-2i\log2,\qquad u=0. \qquad\text{(10)}

Then ℑtan⁡(t/2)=−15/13\Im\tan(t/2)=-15/13, so (9) fails. Nevertheless

H(t,0)=diag⁡(sec⁡(t/2),cos⁡(t/2))(11) H(t,0)= \operatorname{diag}\bigl(\sec(t/2),\cos(t/2)\bigr) \qquad\text{(11)}

has positive real part. In fact (11) has positive real part on the entire strip |ℜt|<π|\Re t|<\pi, at every imaginary height. Its branch is q=sec⁡(t/2)q=\sec(t/2).

The example also lies in the positive single-edge geometric chart: eit/2e^{it/2}, its reciprocal, and sec⁡(t/2)+tan⁡(t/2)\sec(t/2)+\tan(t/2) and its reciprocal all have positive real part. The eight shapes in Section 3 of the isolated-vertex geometry note therefore have positive imaginary part. Thus (9) is not being proposed as a characterization of positive tetrahedra. No equivalence between positivity of all eight shapes and (6) is claimed here.

4. Arbitrary powers and arbitrary connected sets of vertices

Let SS be a finite set of selected vertices in an original circle tree. Include each original edge incident to SS once. At an endpoint outside SS, fix the boundary angle at zero. Let

ΨS(𝒕)=∑vwψ(tv,tw),F𝒏,𝒄(𝒕)=ΨS(𝒕)+i4π∑v∈Snvtv2−i∑v∈Scvtv,(12) \Psi_S(\mathbf t)=\sum_{vw}\psi(t_v,t_w), \qquad F_{\mathbf n,\boldsymbol c}(\mathbf t) =\Psi_S(\mathbf t) +\frac{i}{4\pi}\sum_{v\in S}n_vt_v^2 -i\sum_{v\in S}c_vt_v, \qquad\text{(12)}

where nvn_v are real numbers (in particular, any fixed signed integer powers), and cvc_v are real cone charges. Each selected vertex is assumed to have at least one incident edge. All phases are on one specified lift.

Let Ω⊂ℂS\Omega\subset\mathbb C^S be an open convex region. Suppose that at every point of Ω\Omega, each edge with both endpoints selected satisfies (6) on the continued branch. An edge with only one selected endpoint is required to satisfy the corresponding positive real diagonal test. For instance, these assumptions hold if every edge pair belongs to 𝒞\mathcal C; for an outside endpoint zero, (11) gives the larger strip directly.

Theorem 3. Under these assumptions,

ℜ[−F𝒏,𝒄″]>0on Ω.(13) \Re[-F''_{\mathbf n,\boldsymbol c}]>0 \quad\text{on }\Omega. \qquad\text{(13)}

For every fixed choice of the powers and cone charges, there is at most one critical point of F𝒏,𝒄F_{\mathbf n,\boldsymbol c} in Ω\Omega. If that critical point is z*z_*, its Hessian is nonsingular. On every real horizontal segment z*+sx⊂Ωz_*+sx\subset\Omega, x∈ℝSx\in\mathbb R^S, 0≤s≤10\le s\le1, the real action is strictly smaller than at z*z_*, unless x=0x=0.

If ℜ[−F″]≥γI\Re[-F'']\ge\gamma I on such a segment, then the quantitative comparison is

ℜF(z*+x)≤ℜF(z*)−γ2|x|2.(14) \Re F(z_*+x)\le \Re F(z_*)-\frac{\gamma}{2}|x|^2. \qquad\text{(14)}

Proof. The Hessian of −ΨS-\Psi_S is the sum of the edge matrices H/(2π)H/(2\pi), inserted in their original vertex coordinates. Each summand has positive real part on its endpoint coordinates. Every nonzero vector has a nonzero coordinate at a vertex incident to one of these edges, so the sum is positive definite. The filling quadratic contributes −idiag⁡(nv)/(2π)-i\operatorname{diag}(n_v)/(2\pi); this is skew-Hermitian and contributes zero to the Hermitian part. The charge term contributes no Hessian. This proves (13).

Put G=−∇FG=-\nabla F. If z,w∈Ωz,w\in\Omega and v=z−w≠0v=z-w\ne0, convexity permits integration along the straight segment:

ℜ(v*[G(z)−G(w)])=∫01ℜ(v*DG(w+sv)v)ds>0.(15) \Re\bigl(v^*[G(z)-G(w)]\bigr) =\int_0^1 \Re\bigl(v^*DG(w+sv)v\bigr)\,ds>0. \qquad\text{(15)}

For a complex symmetric matrix the Hermitian part is its entrywise real part, so (13) applies also to complex vectors vv. Thus G(z)≠G(w)G(z)\ne G(w). In particular there cannot be two critical points. If F″v=0F''v=0, (13) applied to vv would give a contradiction, proving nonsingularity.

Finally, along the real horizontal segment the first derivative at s=0s=0 is zero and the second derivative is ℜ(xTF″x)<0\Re(x^TF''x)<0. Twice integrating proves the strict comparison and (14). ▫\square

Convexity of Ω\Omega is an actual hypothesis. Neither 𝒞\mathcal C nor the full locus (6) has been asserted to be convex. One may use convex subsets of these loci, or verify (6) directly on a specified convex region.

5. Which existing geometric paths satisfy the test?

For the regular all-zero subtree geometry, the selected angles are tv=−ihvt_v=-ih_v, with hv≥0h_v\ge0. Write rv=tanh⁡(hv/2)r_v=\tanh(h_v/2). On an edge vwvw, condition (9) becomes

rv2+rw2<1.(16) r_v^2+r_w^2<1. \qquad\text{(16)}

This is exactly the regular-edge condition already used there:

rv2+rw2<1⇔sinh⁡(hv/2)sinh⁡(hw/2)<1.(17) r_v^2+r_w^2<1 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sinh(h_v/2)\sinh(h_w/2)<1. \qquad\text{(17)}

To verify the equivalence, square the nonnegative product and use sinh⁡2(h/2)=r2/(1−r2)\sinh^2(h/2)=r^2/(1-r^2). The resulting inequality is rv2rw2<(1−rv2)(1−rw2)r_v^2r_w^2<(1-r_v^2)(1-r_w^2), which is (16).

Consequently the entire straight-height cone path in the regular zero-subtree geometry note satisfies (13) at its selected point. This includes its uniform sufficient class

tan⁡(π/dv)tan⁡(π/dw)≤1(18) \tan(\pi/d_v)\tan(\pi/d_w)\le1 \qquad\text{(18)}

on each internal selected edge, in particular all selected degrees at least four. The strict endpoint inequalities in that note ensure (17) even when equality holds in (18). This is a conclusion about that path and its small neighborhoods. It does not put an entire period contour inside one common convex region.

The small complex charts in the connected-filling geometry note also satisfy (9). For example, if |t|,|u|≤1/64|t|,|u|\le1/64, then |tan⁡(t/2)|,|tan⁡(u/2)|<1/127|\tan(t/2)|,|\tan(u/2)|<1/127, and the sum in (9) is less than 2/12722/127^2.

For an isolated selected vertex all its neighboring angles are zero. Formula (11) supplies (13) on the entire convex strip |ℜt|<π|\Re t|<\pi, for every signed power nn. The existence theorem in the isolated-vertex note therefore supplies the unique selected critical point in that strip whenever d+|n|>2d+|n|>2. No restriction to large |n||n| or large degree is needed for this uniqueness.

6. The geometric value and what this comparison does not prove

The original peripheral formulas must still be retained. With nv=ηv−2pvn_v=\eta_v-2p_v, where ηv\eta_v is the original carrier and pvp_v is the integer filling coefficient, they are

Uv=−2π(ΨS)tv−iηvtv,Vv=2itv.(19) U_v=-2\pi(\Psi_S)_{t_v}-i\eta_vt_v,\qquad V_v=2it_v. \qquad\text{(19)}

The stationary equations for (12) give the full primitive cone periods

Uv+pvVv=−2πicv.(20) U_v+p_vV_v=-2\pi i c_v. \qquad\text{(20)}

Suppose a marked positive geometric path t(s)t(s), with charges cv(s)c_v(s), has actually been constructed, using the original face pairings and the continued logarithms in (19). Its core lengths are −2ℑtv(s)-2\Im t_v(s). Stationarity then gives

ddsℜF𝒏,𝒄(s)(𝒕(s))=∑vℑtv(s)cv′(s).(21) \frac{d}{ds}\Re F_{\mathbf n,\boldsymbol c(s)}(\mathbf t(s)) =\sum_v\Im t_v(s)\,c_v'(s). \qquad\text{(21)}

Schläfli’s formula for this same cone path gives 2π2\pi times (21) as the volume derivative. Starting with its complete octahedral volume fixes the additive constant. This is the geometric calibration already proved for the paths cited above. It is not a consequence of Hessian positivity alone.

For 0<cv<10<c_v<1, the meridian in (20) generally has nontrivial elliptic holonomy. Its representation is a representation of the unfilled exterior; it need not factor through the group of the smoothly filled manifold. An upper bound for volumes of representations of that smoothly filled manifold therefore does not apply to this intermediate path without another argument.

There are three distinct gaps between Theorem 3 and the required dominant-saddle transport statement:

  1. Other critical points need not lie in the convex region of Theorem 3. Positive shapes of the selected point do not place the other solutions of the gluing equations in the same positive angle domain or logarithmic lift.
  2. A strictly concave horizontal action does not compare that action with critical values or endpoint contributions on other sheets. The adjacent-pair alias identity gives an actual equal-real-action end. Its removal uses the exact source identities, not a strict inequality from geometry.
  3. Even uniqueness of all finite critical points in a region does not prove that the original relative cycle can be deformed into it without additional divisors, residues, or ends. The nonzero coefficient of the selected cycle and estimates on the remaining chains are separate requirements.

Thus the result supplies a general, elementary criterion for local and regional convexity that retains the actual filling powers. For arbitrary fixed short partial fillings, the still missing statement is a global comparison or an exact reduction of the original period to a region where such a comparison applies.
