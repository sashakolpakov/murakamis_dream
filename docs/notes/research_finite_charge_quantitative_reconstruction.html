Quantitative reconstruction of the canonical finite charge source

Private written proof, 16 November 2026.

Two consecutive blocks of lattice charge observations recover the canonical polarized charge function, including its first-normal blocks. For the actual cosine source, its exact charge reflection reduces this to one full block of NN samples. The recovery has polynomial cost on bounded real charge sets and an explicit, sharp exponential cost in a complex direction. It is different from reconstructing a contour-polarized family. The known small-charge initializer does not provide this full unit charge block with the required uniform error.

The signed period difference and its discrete Fourier transform already occur in the actual charge equation, §§12–13. The Fourier–Hermite Gram gives polynomially conditioned physical-jet coordinates, and the one-sided angular projection changes angular representatives. The additional result below is a continuous-charge reconstruction with quantitative error bounds, using the canonical finite charge normal form.

1. The actual charge space and its two sample blocks

Fix N≥2N\ge2, D=N−1D=N-1, η=(−1)D\eta=(-1)^D, and m=⌊N/2⌋m=\lfloor N/2\rfloor. Use y=1−χy=1-\chi and λa=π(D−2a),0≤a≤D.(1) \lambda_a=\pi(D-2a),\qquad 0\le a\le D. \qquad\text{(1)} The canonical polarized observation of an original boundary class f∈𝒜Nf\in\mathcal A_N is Fσ(y)=ϕN(fexp⁡𝒜N(σiNyLN)),σ∈{+1,−1}.(2) F_\sigma(y)=\phi_N\!\left(f\exp_{\mathcal A_N} (\sigma iNyL_N)\right), \qquad \sigma\in\{+1,-1\}. \qquad\text{(2)} The complete finite normal form proved in the polarization source is Fσ(y)=∑a=0m−1(ua+yva)eσiλay+𝟏Noddu*.(3) F_\sigma(y)= \sum_{a=0}^{m-1}(u_a+yv_a)e^{\sigma i\lambda_a y} +\mathbf1_{N\ {\mathrm{ odd}}}u_*. \qquad\text{(3)} All coefficients depend linearly on the full original values and first normals of ff. No normal is omitted: the coefficients of yeσiλayy e^{\sigma i\lambda_a y} recover the values, and the other coefficients then recover the first normals. The odd simple node contributes only u*u_*.

For the cosine observation F(y)=ϕN(f𝒞N,1−y),(4) F(y)=\phi_N(f\mathcal C_{N,1-y}), \qquad\text{(4)} its positive and negative generalized frequencies give exactly Fσ=(2Πσ+Π0)F.(5) F_\sigma=(2\Pi_\sigma+\Pi_0)F. \qquad\text{(5)} Here Πσ\Pi_\sigma retains the frequencies σλa\sigma\lambda_a, 0≤a<m0\le a<m, together with their linear polynomial factors, and Π0\Pi_0 retains the odd constant. This is the actual canonical coupon projection.

Both (3) and (4) belong to the larger space ℬN={∑a=0D(Ua+yVa)eiλay}.(6) \mathcal B_N= \left\{\sum_{a=0}^{D}(U_a+yV_a)e^{i\lambda_a y}\right\}. \qquad\text{(6)} Its dimension is 2N2N. Every F∈ℬNF\in\mathcal B_N obeys B(y):=ηF(y+1)−F(y)=∑aVaeiλay,A(y):=F(y)−yB(y)=∑aUaeiλay.(7) B(y):=\eta F(y+1)-F(y)=\sum_aV_a e^{i\lambda_a y}, \qquad A(y):=F(y)-yB(y)=\sum_aU_a e^{i\lambda_a y}. \qquad\text{(7)} Indeed eiλa=ηe^{i\lambda_a}=\eta. Thus the first difference in (7) retains the normal channel; replacing it by signed periodicity would lose that channel.

Set yj=j/Ny_j=j/N, 0≤j<N0\le j<N, and retain the 2N2N samples dj=F(yj),dj+N=F(yj+1).(8) d_j=F(y_j),\qquad d_{j+N}=F(y_j+1). \qquad\text{(8)} They are the original charge lattice χ=1−j/N\chi=1-j/N, over two consecutive blocks. Define Bj=ηdj+N−dj,Aj=dj−yjBj.(9) B_j=\eta d_{j+N}-d_j,\qquad A_j=d_j-y_jB_j. \qquad\text{(9)} Ordinary finite Fourier orthogonality gives the explicit inverse Va=1N∑j=0N−1e−iλayjBj,Ua=1N∑j=0N−1e−iλayjAj.(10) V_a=\frac1N\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}e^{-i\lambda_a y_j}B_j, \qquad U_a=\frac1N\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}e^{-i\lambda_a y_j}A_j. \qquad\text{(10)} For example, e−iπDyB(y)=∑aVae−2πiaye^{-i\pi Dy}B(y)=\sum_aV_ae^{-2\pi iay}; the NN frequencies are distinct modulo NN. This proves (10), and also proves that (8) determines the whole of (6). There is no division by an unknown source scalar.

For the actual cosine source there is a stronger reconstruction. Its normal form is even in yy, and (7) gives the exact relation F(y+1)+F(y−1)=2ηF(y).(10a) F(y+1)+F(y-1)=2\eta F(y). \qquad\text{(10a)} Consequently the first block alone determines the second: dN=ηd0,dN+j=2ηdj−dN−j(1≤j<N).(10b) d_N=\eta d_0,\qquad d_{N+j}=2\eta d_j-d_{N-j}\quad(1\le j<N). \qquad\text{(10b)} Equivalently these are the full-quotient Chebyshev relations TN+j(−z/2)+TN−j(−z/2)=2ηTj(−z/2)T_{N+j}(-z/2)+T_{N-j}(-z/2)=2\eta T_j(-z/2), since TN(−z/2)=ηT_N(-z/2)=\eta in 𝒜N\mathcal A_N. They preserve the first normals. Substitution into (9) gives B0=0,A0=d0,Bj=dj−ηdN−j,Aj=(1−yj)dj+ηyjdN−j(1≤j<N).(10c) \begin{aligned} &B_0=0,\qquad A_0=d_0,\\ &B_j=d_j-\eta d_{N-j},\qquad A_j=(1-y_j)d_j+\eta y_jd_{N-j}\quad(1\le j<N). \end{aligned} \qquad\text{(10c)} Equations (10), (10c), and (5) reconstruct the canonical polarized observation from the NN original cosine observations at χ=1−j/N\chi=1-j/N, 0≤j<N0\le j<N. Charge zero is already determined by charge one. This does not assert a signed-period law for the normal blocks: the nonzero difference BjB_j is still retained.

2. Exact interpolation and its complex error bound

Define the finite kernels Kσ,N(z)=2∑a=0m−1eσiλaz+𝟏Nodd.(11) K_{\sigma,N}(z)= 2\sum_{a=0}^{m-1}e^{\sigma i\lambda_a z} +\mathbf1_{N\ {\mathrm{ odd}}}. \qquad\text{(11)} Their total coefficient weight is 2m+𝟏Nodd=N2m+\mathbf1_{N\ {\mathrm{ odd}}}=N. Substituting (9) into (10) and applying (5) proves Fσ(y)=1N∑j=0N−1Kσ,N(y−yj)[(1+yj−y)dj+η(y−yj)dj+N]−𝟏NoddyN∑j=0N−1Bj.(12) \boxed{\quad F_\sigma(y)=\frac1N\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}K_{\sigma,N}(y-y_j) \bigl[(1+y_j-y)d_j+\eta(y-y_j)d_{j+N}\bigr] -\mathbf1_{N\ {\mathrm{ odd}}}\frac yN\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}B_j. \quad} \qquad\text{(12)} This formula reconstructs the polarized observation from the cosine observations (8), not merely from already polarized data. The factor two in (11) is absent at the odd simple frequency. The final term projects away the linear zero-frequency coefficient. It is zero for the exact cosine data, since their odd zero-frequency block is constant. Keeping it in (12) makes the reconstruction land in the canonical space even for noisy data. If the supplied data are instead those of (3), the analogous direct reconstruction replaces the factor two by one; it retains the odd constant once.

Let d̂\widehat d be arbitrary approximate samples with max0≤j<2N|d̂j−dj|≤δN.(13) \max_{0\le j<2N}|\widehat d_j-d_j|\le\delta_N. \qquad\text{(13)} Apply the right side of (12) to these data and call its output F̂σ\widehat F_\sigma. Since 0≤yj<10\le y_j<1 and 0<λa≤πD0<\lambda_a\le\pi D for a<ma<m, |Kσ,N(y−yj)|≤NeπD(−σℑy)+,|1+yj−y|+|y−yj|≤3+2|y|.(14) |K_{\sigma,N}(y-y_j)| \le N e^{\pi D(-\sigma\Im y)_+}, \qquad |1+y_j-y|+|y-y_j|\le3+2|y|. \qquad\text{(14)} Here r+=max⁡(r,0)r_+=\max(r,0). At even levels these inequalities immediately bound the reconstruction. At odd levels separate the nonzero frequencies, of total weight N−1N-1, from the corrected constant. The latter is N−1∑jAjN^{-1}\sum_j A_j, whose error is at most 3δN3\delta_N. Thus at either parity |F̂σ(y)−Fσ(y)|≤N(3+2|y|)δNeπD(−σℑy)+.(15) \boxed{\quad |\widehat F_\sigma(y)-F_\sigma(y)| \le N(3+2|y|)\delta_N e^{\pi D(-\sigma\Im y)_+}. \quad} \qquad\text{(15)} The same bound holds for the direct reconstruction from polarized samples. Equations (9)–(10) also give the separate coefficient bounds |ΔVa|≤2δN,|ΔUa|≤3δN.(16) |\Delta V_a|\le2\delta_N,\qquad |\Delta U_a|\le3\delta_N. \qquad\text{(16)} All formulas hold at both parities, including N=2N=2. They hold with coefficients in any normed vector space, by the same triangle inequalities; in particular ordinary free-boundary parameters and fixed derivatives may be retained.

For the actual cosine source, suppose instead that only its first block is approximated, with error at most δN\delta_N. Construct Aj,BjA_j,B_j by (10c) before applying (10) and (5). Their errors satisfy |ΔBj|≤2δN,|ΔAj|≤δN,(16a) |\Delta B_j|\le2\delta_N,\qquad |\Delta A_j|\le\delta_N, \qquad\text{(16a)} because the two coefficients of AjA_j have absolute values 1−yj,yj1-y_j,y_j. The Fourier coefficients have the same bounds. The projection in (5) has total coefficient weight NN, so |ΔFσ(y)|≤N(1+2|y|)δNeπD(−σℑy)+.(16b) \boxed{\quad |\Delta F_\sigma(y)|\le N(1+2|y|)\delta_N e^{\pi D(-\sigma\Im y)_+}. \quad} \qquad\text{(16b)} At odd levels η=1\eta=1 and ∑jBj=0\sum_jB_j=0 follows from j↔N−jj\leftrightarrow N-j, even for the approximate first block. Thus no artificial linear zero-frequency term enters this single-block reconstruction.

The real sample block may start at any real y0y_0: apply the same argument to F(y0+z)F(y_0+z). This translation preserves the frequency subspaces and their projection, so (15) then has 3+2|y−y0|3+2|y-y_0| in place of 3+2|y|3+2|y|. Only the location, not the length, of the required doubled block has changed. This translated assertion uses two blocks; the one-block reduction (10c) uses the original reflection center y=0y=0.

For ss selected variables, use the tensor product of (12). It reconstructs the original ss-variable canonical polarized observation from (2N)s(2N)^s cosine lattice samples, retaining all mixed normal data. If their uniform error is δN\delta_N, then |ΔF𝝈(𝒚)|≤NsδN∏v=1s(3+2|yv|)exp⁡[πD∑v=1s(−σvℑyv)+].(17) |\Delta F_{\boldsymbol\sigma}(\mathbf y)| \le N^s\delta_N\prod_{v=1}^s(3+2|y_v|) \exp\!\left[\pi D\sum_{v=1}^s(-\sigma_v\Im y_v)_+\right]. \qquad\text{(17)} This follows either by multiplying the exact interpolation weights and summing their absolute values, or by successive operator norms from (15). It does not assume independent physical contractions. In charge variables the exponential cost is πD∑v(σvℑχv)+\pi D\sum_v(\sigma_v\Im\chi_v)_+. For the actual tensor cosine source, its separate charge reflection permits (10c) in every coordinate. Only NsN^s samples in {χv=1−jv/N:0≤jv<N}\{\chi_v=1-j_v/N:0\le j_v<N\} are then required, and (17) improves to |ΔF𝝈(𝒚)|≤NsδN∏v=1s(1+2|yv|)exp⁡[πD∑v(−σvℑyv)+].(17a) |\Delta F_{\boldsymbol\sigma}(\mathbf y)| \le N^s\delta_N\prod_{v=1}^s(1+2|y_v|) \exp\!\left[\pi D\sum_v(-\sigma_v\Im y_v)_+\right]. \qquad\text{(17a)} This estimate retains every mixed first normal. The first block in this assertion is the full unit-charge cube, not a fixed small subinterval of it.

3. What the bounds transfer, and what they cost

On a fixed compact target set, suppose the approximate samples have the actual uniform error δN≤CNpeN(M−γ).(18) \delta_N\le C N^p e^{N(M-\gamma)}. \qquad\text{(18)} Then (17) proves the concrete reconstruction error estimate |ΔF𝝈(𝒚)|≤C′Np+sexp⁡N[M−γ+π∑v(−σvℑyv)+].(19) |\Delta F_{\boldsymbol\sigma}(\mathbf y)| \le C' N^{p+s} \exp N\left[M-\gamma+ \pi\sum_v(-\sigma_v\Im y_v)_+\right]. \qquad\text{(19)} Thus comparison with a proposed target action S(𝒚)S(\mathbf y) has a strict relative error gap whenever γ>M+π∑v(−σvℑyv)+−ℜS(𝒚)(20) \gamma>M+\pi\sum_v(-\sigma_v\Im y_v)_+ -\Re S(\mathbf y) \qquad\text{(20)} uniformly on that set. The reconstructed approximate function is specified by (12); identifying its asymptotic action is a separate calculation. Formula (19) does not assume that interpolating a lattice leading term automatically produces eNSe^{NS}. Nor does it supply the sample error (18).

For an actual heat preimage with real base power bvb_v, target power nvn_v, and target angle tv*t_v^*, substitute χ0,v=1−(nv−bv)tv*2π,y0,v=(nv−bv)tv*2π(21) \chi_{0,v}=1-\frac{(n_v-b_v)t_v^*}{2\pi},\qquad y_{0,v}=\frac{(n_v-b_v)t_v^*}{2\pi} \qquad\text{(21)} into (19). Formula (21) follows also by subtracting the original primitive equations at powers 𝒃\mathbf b and 𝒏\mathbf n, at the same angle: P𝒃=P𝒏+i(𝒏−𝒃)𝒕*/2P_{\mathbf b}=P_{\mathbf n} +i(\mathbf n-\mathbf b)\mathbf t^*/2, coordinatewise. Its explicit directional cost is π∑v(−σvℑy0,v)+=π∑v(σvℑχ0,v)+=12∑v[−σv(nv−bv)ℑtv*]+.(22) \pi\sum_v(-\sigma_v\Im y_{0,v})_+ =\pi\sum_v(\sigma_v\Im\chi_{0,v})_+ =\frac12\sum_v[-\sigma_v(n_v-b_v)\Im t_v^*]_+. \qquad\text{(22)} For instance a uniform absolute sample error e−Nγe^{-N\gamma} is still exponentially small after this reconstruction if γ\gamma exceeds (22). Any subsequent heat integral retains its own marked Gaussian and tail estimates; these are not included in (22). This is a quantitative prerequisite, not coverage of the preimages by the known small-charge samples.

The exponential factor in (15) cannot be removed from a uniform reconstruction theorem. Take the actual cosine-space element F(y)=cos⁡(πDy)F(y)=\cos(\pi Dy). Its real lattice samples have modulus at most one, whereas (5) gives Fσ(y)=eσiπDy,|Fσ(y)|=eπD(−σℑy)if −σℑy>0.(23) F_\sigma(y)=e^{\sigma i\pi Dy},\qquad |F_\sigma(y)|=e^{\pi D(-\sigma\Im y)} \quad\text{if }-\sigma\Im y>0. \qquad\text{(23)} This is a member of the faithful finite source space, so the exponential rate in (15) is sharp. It is not an assertion that this particular boundary class is a prescribed knot numerator. The polynomial factor in (15) is not claimed optimal.

There is also exponential instability when only a short real charge interval is supplied. Let N≥4N\ge4, 0<L<10<L<1, and I=[yc−L/2,yc+L/2]I=[y_c-L/2,y_c+L/2]. The function GN(y)=eσiπDy(1−e−σ2πi(y−yc))m−1(24) G_N(y)=e^{\sigma i\pi Dy} \bigl(1-e^{-\sigma2\pi i(y-y_c)}\bigr)^{m-1} \qquad\text{(24)} lies in the span of pure exponentials in (3): its binomial expansion uses precisely the allowed frequencies σλa\sigma\lambda_a, 0≤a<m0\le a<m. For a real target y*y_* with |sin⁡π(y*−yc)|>sin⁡(πL/2),r=sin⁡(πL/2)|sin⁡π(y*−yc)|<1,(25) |\sin\pi(y_*-y_c)|>\sin(\pi L/2), \qquad r=\frac{\sin(\pi L/2)} {|\sin\pi(y_*-y_c)|}<1, \qquad\text{(25)} one has supy∈I|GN(y)GN(y*)|≤rm−1,GN(y*)GN(y*)=1.(26) \sup_{y\in I}\left|\frac{G_N(y)}{G_N(y_*)}\right| \le r^{m-1}, \qquad \frac{G_N(y_*)}{G_N(y_*)}=1. \qquad\text{(26)} Indeed |1−eiθ|=2|sin⁡(θ/2)||1-e^{i\theta}|=2|\sin(\theta/2)|, and the carrier has modulus one on the real line. Thus even samples at every lattice point of II cannot give a uniform polynomial-cost extrapolation to y*y_* on this exact finite space: the norm of any exact linear reconstruction is at least r−(m−1)r^{-(m-1)}. The same two-data-set uncertainty applies to reconstruction from inexact samples. This is an observation-operator estimate, not an actual-knot cancellation example.

4. The finite-space restriction and the height-current distinction

The canonical functions (3) have at most linear growth on the real yy-axis. Their frequencies are one-sided and their maximal frequency is π(N−1)\pi(N-1), strictly below the lattice Nyquist frequency πN\pi N. Reconstruction above uses the exact physical frequency set and its size-two normal blocks, not an unsupported claim that exponential type alone characterizes those functions.

For comparison, the exact full-midpoint zero-power height current from the height-transport note is Jσ,0,1−yh[A]=σηN22πA♯(2cosh⁡h)sinh⁡hcosh⁡(Nyh)cosh⁡2(Nh/2)sin⁡(πNy).(27) J_{\sigma,0,1-y}^{h}[A] =\frac{\sigma\eta N^2}{2\pi}A^\sharp(2\cosh h) \frac{\sinh h\cosh(Nyh)}{\cosh^2(Nh/2)} \sin(\pi Ny). \qquad\text{(27)} It vanishes at every sample y=j/Ny=j/N. When h>0h>0 and its displayed spectator value is nonzero, it is not in (3) or (6): along real yy, the factor cosh⁡(Nyh)\cosh(Nyh) has exponential growth on subsequences with |sin⁡(πNy)|=1|\sin(\pi Ny)|=1. It also contains the excluded Nyquist sine factor. Thus (12) does not identify different contour heights off the lattice. A restriction to the canonical finite charge space distinguishes this exact current, but does not prove that the contour-polarized initializer belongs to that space.

The existing local small-charge asymptotic supplies neither the full unit block in (10c) nor the action-relative error required by (20). The positive zero-subtree theorem at fixed selected charge one supplies a different boundary-angle family. None of these facts is replaced by (12). What is established here is a stable full-normal finite reconstruction and its explicit continuation cost, available if the required actual charge samples are controlled.
