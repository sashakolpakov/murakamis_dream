Positive edge geometry and global uniqueness of the filling equations

Research note, 2 November 2026. The eight original tetrahedra of a circle-tree edge determine a domain on which its peripheral-period map is globally strictly monotone. This statement compares any two points of the domain; it does not require the segment between them to remain positive. Summing the comparison over the original graph proves uniqueness of a positive solution of the primitive filling equations, for arbitrary signed powers.

Existence is a different question. The last section identifies what can obstruct continuation of the positive solution. Neither uniqueness nor existence by itself determines the coefficient of its cycle in the original quantum period.

1. The original shapes in rational coordinates

Retain the notation of the Hessian calculation:

a=tan⁡(t/2),b=tan⁡(u/2),q2=1+a2+b2,q(0,0)=1.(1) a=\tan(t/2),\qquad b=\tan(u/2),\qquad q^2=1+a^2+b^2, \qquad q(0,0)=1. \qquad\text{(1)}

The original meridian increments are

rt=arsinh⁡(tan⁡(t/2)cos⁡(u/2)),ru=arsinh⁡(tan⁡(u/2)cos⁡(t/2)).(2) r_t=\operatorname{arsinh}\bigl(\tan(t/2)\cos(u/2)\bigr), \qquad r_u=\operatorname{arsinh}\bigl(\tan(u/2)\cos(t/2)\bigr). \qquad\text{(2)}

They satisfy ψt=−rt/π\psi_t=-r_t/\pi and ψu=−ru/π\psi_u=-r_u/\pi. All logarithms initially vanish at completeness. Their global positive branches will be specified below.

The ordered cross-ratio calculation in Section 3 of the adjacent-pair geometry note gives the eight original shapes as

(A,B,−1/A,−1/B),(C,D,−1/C,−1/D).(3) (A,B,-1/A,-1/B),\qquad(C,D,-1/C,-1/D). \qquad\text{(3)}

The four distinct expressions have the following rational form:

A=q+ab−i,C=q−ab−i,B=−q−ba+i,D=−q+ba+i.(4) A=\frac{q+a}{b-i},\qquad C=\frac{q-a}{b-i},\qquad B=-\frac{q-b}{a+i},\qquad D=-\frac{q+b}{a+i}. \qquad\text{(4)}

To check the conventions, the original formulas are A=ierte−iu/2A=i e^{r_t}e^{-iu/2}, C=ie−rte−iu/2C=i e^{-r_t}e^{-iu/2}, B=ieit/2e−ruB=i e^{it/2}e^{-r_u}, and D=ieit/2eruD=i e^{it/2}e^{r_u}. The identities ert=cos⁡(u/2)(q+a)e^{r_t}=\cos(u/2)(q+a), eru=cos⁡(t/2)(q+b)e^{r_u}=\cos(t/2)(q+b), and (q−a)(q+a)=1+b2(q-a)(q+a)=1+b^2 give (4). Every expression is ii at completeness. Thus these are the original oriented tetrahedra, with the original two-octahedron subdivision.

Throughout the proof a positive shape means a shape with strictly positive imaginary part. Since z↦−1/zz\mapsto-1/z preserves the upper half-plane, positivity of (3) is equivalent to positivity of the four expressions in (4).

2. Positivity is an exact matrix inequality

Put

L=(q+abbq−a),det⁡L=1.(5) L=\begin{pmatrix}q+a&b\\b&q-a\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det L=1. \qquad\text{(5)}

For a complex symmetric matrix, ℜL\Re L is both its entrywise real part and its Hermitian part.

Theorem 1. At a regular configuration in (1)–(4), all eight original tetrahedra are positive if and only if

ℜL>0,equivalentlyℜq>(ℜa)2+(ℜb)2.(6) \Re L>0, \quad\text{equivalently}\quad \Re q>\sqrt{(\Re a)^2+(\Re b)^2}. \qquad\text{(6)}

This is also equivalent to ℜ[−2πψ″(t,u)]>0\Re[-2\pi\psi''(t,u)]>0 on the branch continued from the positive complete configuration.

Proof. Write r=q+ar=q+a and

k=(ℜr)(ℑb)−(ℑr)(ℜb). k=(\Re r)(\Im b)-(\Im r)(\Re b).

The determinant identity in (5) gives q−a=(1+b2)/rq-a=(1+b^2)/r. In particular C=(b+i)/rC=(b+i)/r. Direct division in (4) gives

ℑA=ℜr−k|b−i|2,ℑC=ℜr+k|r|2.(7) \Im A=\frac{\Re r-k}{|b-i|^2}, \qquad \Im C=\frac{\Re r+k}{|r|^2}. \qquad\text{(7)}

Also

det⁡(ℜL)=(ℜr)2−k2|r|2.(8) \det(\Re L)= \frac{(\Re r)^2-k^2}{|r|^2}. \qquad\text{(8)}

For completeness, write r=x+iyr=x+iy, b=B+iDb=B+iD. Then |r|2[xℜ1+b2r−B2]=x2−(xD−yB)2, |r|^2\left[x\,\Re\frac{1+b^2}{r}-B^2\right] =x^2-(xD-yB)^2, which is (8).

Equations (7)–(8) show that A,CA,C are both positive exactly when the first diagonal entry of ℜL\Re L and its determinant are positive. This is equivalent to ℜL>0\Re L>0. Applying the same calculation with (a,b)(a,b) replaced by (−b,−a)(-b,-a) gives B,DB,D. The real matrices in the two calculations have the same eigenvalues

ℜq±(ℜa)2+(ℜb)2. \Re q\ \pm\sqrt{(\Re a)^2+(\Re b)^2}.

Thus positivity of A,CA,C also implies positivity of B,DB,D, and proves (6).

To compare with the Hessian, write P=ℜqP=\Re q, Q=ℑqQ=\Im q, X=(ℜa)2+(ℜb)2X=(\Re a)^2+(\Re b)^2, Y=(ℑa)2+(ℑb)2Y=(\Im a)^2+(\Im b)^2, and Z=(ℜa)(ℑa)+(ℜb)(ℑb)Z=(\Re a)(\Im a)+(\Re b)(\Im b). The identity q2=1+a2+b2q^2=1+a^2+b^2 gives

P2−Q2=1+X−Y,PQ=Z. P^2-Q^2=1+X-Y,\qquad PQ=Z.

Since {ℑ(ab¯)}2=XY−Z2\{\Im(a\overline b)\}^2=XY-Z^2, it follows that

P2−{ℑ(ab¯)}2=(P2−X)(1+X+Q2).(9) P^2-\{\Im(a\overline b)\}^2 =(P^2-X)(1+X+Q^2). \qquad\text{(9)}

The determinant formula in Proposition 1 of the Hessian note therefore reads

det⁡ℜ[−2πψ″]=(P2−X)(1+X+Q2)|q|2.(10) \det\Re[-2\pi\psi''] =\frac{(P^2-X)(1+X+Q^2)}{|q|^2}. \qquad\text{(10)}

The corresponding trace is P(1+|q|−2)P(1+|q|^{-2}). Consequently its real part is positive definite exactly when P>XP>\sqrt X, proving the last assertion. ▫\square

3. Global coordinates: two disks and two strips

The Cayley transform of (5) is

CL=(L−I)(L+I)−1=1q+1(abb−a).(11) C_L=(L-I)(L+I)^{-1} =\frac1{q+1}\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\b&-a\end{pmatrix}. \qquad\text{(11)}

The notation CLC_L distinguishes this matrix from the tetrahedron shape CC. Direct multiplication gives

I−CL*CL=4(L+I)−*(ℜL)(L+I)−1.(12) I-C_L^*C_L =4(L+I)^{-*}(\Re L)(L+I)^{-1}. \qquad\text{(12)}

Set

ζ=a+ibq+1,ξ=a−ibq+1.(13) \zeta=\frac{a+ib}{q+1},\qquad \xi=\frac{a-ib}{q+1}. \qquad\text{(13)}

The two eigenvalues of CL*CLC_L^*C_L are |ζ|2,|ξ|2|\zeta|^2,|\xi|^2: its equal diagonal entries are (|a|2+|b|2)/|q+1|2(|a|^2+|b|^2)/|q+1|^2, and the off-diagonal entry is (a¯b−b¯a)/|q+1|2(\overline a b-\overline b a)/|q+1|^2. Thus (6) is exactly

|ζ|<1,|ξ|<1.(14) |\zeta|<1,\qquad|\xi|<1. \qquad\text{(14)}

Conversely, any pair in these two disks gives

q=1+ζξ1−ζξ,a=ζ+ξ1−ζξ,b=ζ−ξi(1−ζξ).(15) q=\frac{1+\zeta\xi}{1-\zeta\xi},\qquad a=\frac{\zeta+\xi}{1-\zeta\xi},\qquad b=\frac{\zeta-\xi}{i(1-\zeta\xi)}. \qquad\text{(15)}

The denominator never vanishes, and (12) proves positivity. This parametrizes the entire positive edge domain, not only a neighborhood of completeness.

Now use the principal strip coordinates

P=artanh⁡ζ,Q=artanh⁡ξ,|ℑP|,|ℑQ|<π4.(16) P=\operatorname{artanh}\zeta,\qquad Q=\operatorname{artanh}\xi,\qquad |\Im P|,|\Im Q|<\frac{\pi}{4}. \qquad\text{(16)}

The function artanh⁡z=12Log⁡((1+z)/(1−z))\operatorname{artanh} z= \tfrac12\operatorname{Log}((1+z)/(1-z)) maps the unit disk bijectively onto this horizontal strip. Define

f(P)=arctan⁡(tanh⁡P).(17) f(P)=\arctan(\tanh P). \qquad\text{(17)}

Here the inverse tangent is the analytic branch at zero on the unit disk. It satisfies

f′(P)=1cosh⁡(2P),ℜf′(P)>0(|ℑP|<π/4).(18) f'(P)=\frac1{\cosh(2P)},\qquad \Re f'(P)>0\quad (|\Im P|<\pi/4). \qquad\text{(18)}

Indeed ℜcosh⁡(2P)=cosh⁡(2ℜP)cos⁡(2ℑP)>0\Re\cosh(2P)=\cosh(2\Re P)\cos(2\Im P)>0, and taking a reciprocal preserves the sign of the real part.

The original angles and meridian increments are exactly

t=2{f(P)+f(Q)},u=−2i(P−Q),rt=P+Q,ru=i{f(Q)−f(P)}.(19) \begin{aligned} t&=2\{f(P)+f(Q)\},& u&=-2i(P-Q),\\ r_t&=P+Q,& r_u&=i\{f(Q)-f(P)\}. \end{aligned} \qquad\text{(19)}

One can verify the angle identities before taking logarithms:

eit=(1+iζ)(1+iξ)(1−iζ)(1−iξ),eiu=(1+ζ)(1−ξ)(1−ζ)(1+ξ).(20) e^{it}= \frac{(1+i\zeta)(1+i\xi)} {(1-i\zeta)(1-i\xi)},\qquad e^{iu}= \frac{(1+\zeta)(1-\xi)} {(1-\zeta)(1+\xi)}. \qquad\text{(20)}

All four one-variable factors in these quotients have analytic logarithms on the disks. They fix (19) without an integer ambiguity. Likewise e2rt=(1+ζ)(1+ξ)(1−ζ)(1−ξ),e2ru=(1−iζ)(1+iξ)(1+iζ)(1−iξ) e^{2r_t}=\frac{(1+\zeta)(1+\xi)} {(1-\zeta)(1-\xi)},\qquad e^{2r_u}=\frac{(1-i\zeta)(1+i\xi)} {(1+i\zeta)(1-i\xi)} give the last two identities in (19).

These are the branches continued from t=u=0t=u=0. They satisfy

|ℜt|,|ℜu|<π,|ℑrt|,|ℑru|<π/2.(21) |\Re t|,|\Re u|<\pi,\qquad |\Im r_t|,|\Im r_u|<\pi/2. \qquad\text{(21)}

For example, |ℜf(P)|<π/4|\Re f(P)|<\pi/4 because f(P)=−iartanh⁡(itanh⁡P)f(P)=-i\operatorname{artanh}(i\tanh P). This proves the first and the last bounds in (21); the other two follow directly from (16).

The positive domain in the original t,ut,u variables is not convex. The two configurations

(−4ilog⁡3,0),(0,−4ilog⁡3)(22) (-4i\log3,0),\qquad (0,-4i\log3) \qquad\text{(22)}

are positive: the nonzero tangent is −40i/41-40i/41 and q=9/41q=9/41. At their midpoint, however, a=b=−4i/5a=b=-4i/5 and q2=−7/25q^2=-7/25, so (6) is impossible. The strip coordinates, rather than a nonexistent convexity of the t,ut,u domain, are essential in the following comparison.

There is nevertheless a contraction to completeness in the tangent variables. Write X=(ℜa)2+(ℜb)2,Y=(ℑa)2+(ℑb)2,Z=(ℜa)(ℑa)+(ℜb)(ℑb),D02=XY−Z2. X=(\Re a)^2+(\Re b)^2,\quad Y=(\Im a)^2+(\Im b)^2,\quad Z=(\Re a)(\Im a)+(\Re b)(\Im b),\quad D_0^2=XY-Z^2. For X>0X>0, condition (6) is equivalent to D02<XD_0^2<X. Indeed, writing q=P0+iQ0q=P_0+iQ_0, the identity X−D02=(P02−X)(X+Q02) X-D_0^2=(P_0^2-X)(X+Q_0^2) proves the equivalence whenever the positive square root exists. Conversely, D02<XD_0^2<X rules out a nonpositive real value of q2q^2: such a value would require Z=0Z=0 and Y≥1+XY\ge1+X, hence D02=XY≥XD_0^2=XY\ge X. For X=0X=0, positivity is simply Y<1Y<1.

Under (a,b)↦(sa,sb)(a,b)\mapsto(sa,sb), 0<s≤10<s\le1, the first criterion becomes s4D02<s2Xs^4D_0^2<s^2X, and the second becomes s2Y<1s^2Y<1. Both are preserved. Positive edges exclude the inverse-tangent cuts a∈i[1,∞)∪−i[1,∞)a\in i[1,\infty)\cup-i[1,\infty), and similarly for bb. For example, if ℜa=0\Re a=0, |ℑa|≥1|\Im a|\ge1, and ℜb≠0\Re b\ne0, then D02≥XD_0^2\ge X; if ℜb=0\Re b=0, then Y≥1Y\ge1. The contraction therefore gives continuous principal angles tv(s)=2arctan⁡(stan⁡(tv/2))t_v(s)=2\arctan(s\tan(t_v/2)).

This fixes a common complete continuation of the positive edge branches on the entire network. The contraction is not generally a path of solutions of fixed filling equations or a path with prescribed cone charges.

The original face chart has no further singularity hidden by the simplified shapes. Its coordinates are E=q+iq−i,R=1+b2q2+1,R1=1−R=1+a2q2+1,Z1=q+bq−i. E=\frac{q+i}{q-i},\qquad R=\frac{1+b^2}{q^2+1},\qquad R_1=1-R=\frac{1+a^2}{q^2+1},\qquad Z_1=\frac{q+b}{q-i}. On the positive domain, qq, q±iq\pm i, q±aq\pm a, and q±bq\pm b are nonzero: the first follows from ℜq>0\Re q>0, and the last two pairs have positive real parts by (6). Together with the positive shapes, these identities verify that the original ordered face formulas remain regular throughout this continuation.

4. Global strict monotonicity of the edge periods

Theorem 2. Let (t,u)(t,u) and (t̃,ũ)(\widetilde t,\widetilde u) be any two positive edge configurations, with the canonical branches in (19). Use δ\delta to denote the difference of the corresponding quantities. Then

ℜ{δrtδt¯+δruδu¯}>0(23) \Re\{\delta r_t\,\overline{\delta t} +\delta r_u\,\overline{\delta u}\}>0 \qquad\text{(23)}

unless the configurations are identical.

Proof. For any two points P,P̃P,\widetilde P in the convex strip (16), integration along their straight segment and (18) give

ℜ{(P−P̃)f(P)−f(P̃)¯}=|P−P̃|2∫01ℜf′(P̃+s(P−P̃))ds>0(24) \Re\{(P-\widetilde P) \overline{f(P)-f(\widetilde P)}\} =|P-\widetilde P|^2 \int_0^1\Re f'(\widetilde P+s(P-\widetilde P))\,ds>0 \qquad\text{(24)}

when P≠P̃P\ne\widetilde P. The same holds for QQ. Substitution of (19) into the left-hand side of (23) gives the exact identity

ℜ{δrtδt¯+δruδu¯}=4ℜ{δPδf(P)¯+δQδf(Q)¯}.(25) \begin{split} &\Re\{\delta r_t\,\overline{\delta t} +\delta r_u\,\overline{\delta u}\}\\ &\hspace{0.8cm}=4\Re\{\delta P\,\overline{\delta f(P)} +\delta Q\,\overline{\delta f(Q)}\}. \end{split} \qquad\text{(25)}

The mixed terms cancel in their real parts. The right side is strictly positive unless both strip coordinates agree. Equations (15) and (19) then show that the edge configurations agree. ▫\square

Thus the period map is globally strictly monotone even though its domain in the original angle coordinates is not convex. The infinitesimal version of (25) recovers the Hessian positivity in Theorem 1.

5. Arbitrary original signed filling powers

Let SS be any selected set of vertices of the original ordered circle tree. The argument also applies to any finite network of these same edge blocks with the same peripheral concatenation rules. Include each edge incident to SS once, and fix the angles at its outside endpoints. Every selected vertex is assumed incident to at least one edge.

Orient each edge only to specify which endpoint is called tt in (19). At a selected vertex define

Rv(𝒕)=∑e∋vrv,e(𝒕).(26) R_v(\mathbf t)=\sum_{e\ni v}r_{v,e}(\mathbf t). \qquad\text{(26)}

For the general-network extension this sum is over endpoint incidences: a loop at vv contributes both its endpoint increments, and contributes two to dvd_v. Original circle trees have no loops.

The original primitive filling equations, with real powers nvn_v and real cone charges cvc_v, are

Rv(𝒕)−inv2tv=−πicv.(27) R_v(\mathbf t)-\frac{i n_v}{2}t_v =-\pi i c_v. \qquad\text{(27)}

For the original integer surgery data, nv=ηv−2pvn_v=\eta_v-2p_v, and

Uv=2Rv−iηvtv,Vv=2itv,Uv+pvVv=−2πicv.(28) U_v=2R_v-i\eta_vt_v,\qquad V_v=2it_v,\qquad U_v+p_vV_v=-2\pi i c_v. \qquad\text{(28)}

These are the full marked logarithmic periods, not merely their exponentials.

Theorem 3. With the outside angles, powers, and charges fixed, equations (27) have at most one solution for which every original edge block is positive on the branches (19). Any such solution has nonsingular complex Jacobian.

Proof. Suppose that two positive solutions exist. Apply (23) to each edge and sum. At an outside endpoint δtv=0\delta t_v=0, so its contribution vanishes. At selected vertices the resulting sum is

ℜ∑v∈SδRvδtv¯.(29) \Re\sum_{v\in S} \delta R_v\,\overline{\delta t_v}. \qquad\text{(29)}

Subtracting (27) gives δRv=invδtv/2\delta R_v=i n_v\delta t_v/2. Since nvn_v is real, every summand in (29) has zero real part. On the other hand, Theorem 2 makes the sum strictly positive if any edge configuration differs. Hence all edge configurations, and therefore all selected angles, agree.

For the Jacobian, each edge derivative is H/2H/2, with positive definite real part by Theorem 1. Insert these matrices in the original vertex coordinates and sum. The real part is positive definite because each selected vertex is incident to an edge. Subtracting idiag⁡(nv)/2i\operatorname{diag}(n_v)/2 leaves that real part unchanged. A complex matrix with positive definite Hermitian part has no nonzero kernel. ▫\square

The assertion permits adjacent selected vertices, arbitrarily many of them, and powers of either sign and any magnitude. It is a theorem about the actual primitive equations in this positive edge decomposition. It does not assert that every hyperbolic filling admits that decomposition with all its original tetrahedra positive.

6. Eliminating a positive filled region preserves the comparison

Divide the vertices of an incident-edge network into interior vertices SS, whose powers and charges are fixed, and boundary vertices BB, whose angles are allowed to vary. Assume every boundary vertex is incident to an included edge. Whenever the interior equations have a positive solution, define the boundary response by

ℛb(𝒕B)=∑e∋brb,e(𝒕S(𝒕B),𝒕B).(32) \mathcal R_b(\mathbf t_B) =\sum_{e\ni b}r_{b,e}(\mathbf t_S(\mathbf t_B), \mathbf t_B). \qquad\text{(32)}

Theorem 3 makes the interior solution unique. Its nonsingular Jacobian makes it holomorphic locally. These local solutions agree on overlaps, so (32) is a well-defined holomorphic response on its realized positive domain.

Corollary 4. For any two different boundary assignments in that domain,

ℜ∑b∈Bδℛbδtb¯>0.(33) \Re\sum_{b\in B} \delta\mathcal R_b\,\overline{\delta t_b}>0. \qquad\text{(33)}

The local derivative ℛ′\mathcal R' is complex symmetric and has positive definite real part.

Proof. Sum (23) over the original edges for the two interior solutions. At each interior vertex, δRv=invδtv/2\delta R_v=i n_v\delta t_v/2, so the real part of its contribution is zero. The remaining terms give (33). If any boundary angle differs, at least one incident edge configuration differs, making the comparison strict.

For the infinitesimal assertion, order the variables as interior followed by boundary. The derivative of the interior equations and boundary response before elimination is a complex symmetric matrix

Hall=(AB0B0TD),ℜHall>0.(34) H_{\mathrm{ all}}= \begin{pmatrix}A&B_0\\B_0^T&D\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \Re H_{\mathrm{ all}}>0. \qquad\text{(34)}

Here AA includes the interior terms −idiag⁡(nv)/2-i\operatorname{diag}(n_v)/2. Implicit differentiation gives the Schur complement

ℛ′=D−B0TA−1B0.(35) \mathcal R'=D-B_0^TA^{-1}B_0. \qquad\text{(35)}

For any nonzero boundary vector yy, put x=−A−1B0yx=-A^{-1}B_0y. Direct multiplication gives

ℜ[y*(D−B0TA−1B0)y]=ℜ[(xy)*Hall(xy)]>0.(36) \Re\left[y^*(D-B_0^TA^{-1}B_0)y\right] =\Re\left[ \begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}^{\!*} H_{\mathrm{ all}} \begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}\right]>0. \qquad\text{(36)}

The Schur complement is symmetric, proving the assertion. ▫\square

An original boundary carrier contributes a purely imaginary real-coefficient multiple of its own angle to the peripheral period. Adding this term preserves (33) and the positive real part in (35). Each original carrier is added once, rather than once for each block incident to its circle.

Consequently any number of positive regions can be eliminated and then joined through their original shared boundary angles: their monotonicity comparisons add, and further real signed filling powers contribute zero real energy. This is a closure property of the actual geometric period equations. It does not assert a corresponding closure theorem for the quantum integration cycles, estimates on other critical points, or nonvanishing leading coefficients.

7. Continuation and its remaining obstruction

At the complete point, with all outside angles zero and all charges zero, Rv=0R_v=0 and the Jacobian of (27) is

12diag⁡(dv−inv),(37) \frac12\operatorname{diag}(d_v-i n_v), \qquad\text{(37)}

where dvd_v is the original degree. It is invertible. Thus any fixed real powers admit a unique positive solution for sufficiently small cone charges.

More generally, follow a continuous prescribed charge path through positive solutions. Theorem 3 rules out both an interior collision of solutions and a singular Jacobian. If the solutions stay in a compact subset of the positive domain, the implicit-function theorem extends them past any finite endpoint of the charge interval. Therefore a maximal positive continuation can stop only by leaving every compact subset of that domain. In the disk coordinates this means approaching a boundary |ζe|=1|\zeta_e|=1 or |ξe|=1|\xi_e|=1, possibly with divergent logarithmic coordinates or degenerate peripheral data.

There are immediate necessary range conditions. From (21) and (27),

|cv|<dv+|nv|2.(38) |c_v|<\frac{d_v+|n_v|}{2}. \qquad\text{(38)}

Indeed, π|cv|≤|ℑRv|+|nv||ℜtv|/2\pi|c_v|\le|\Im R_v|+|n_v|\,|\Re t_v|/2, and each incident meridian increment has imaginary part strictly smaller than π/2\pi/2. At unit charge (38) gives dv+|nv|>2d_v+|n_v|>2. This condition is sufficient in the isolated-vertex theorem. No sufficiency for a connected selected set is claimed here.

The comparison (23) concerns the positive geometric branch. Other critical points of the complexified original source can lie outside this positive domain or on different logarithmic lifts. Neither Theorem 3 nor the continuation statement compares their critical values. The global relative-cycle and endpoint requirements in the persistence theorem therefore remain to be proved separately.

8. Related variational results

Existence and uniqueness through geometric variational principles have substantial precedent. Bobenko and Springborn, Variational principles for circle patterns and Koebe’s theorem, Trans. Amer. Math. Soc. 356 (2004), 659–689, establish existence and uniqueness results for circle patterns with prescribed intersection angles. Rivin’s Combinatorial optimization in geometry provides related optimization background, including applications to hyperbolic structures on three-manifolds.

The statements proved here retain the original complex primitive periods and arbitrary real filling powers, and include the boundary response after eliminating a filled region. Their proofs are the explicit shape identities and monotonicity calculations above. This brief coverage check does not establish a novelty claim or identify these statements with a theorem in either cited work.
