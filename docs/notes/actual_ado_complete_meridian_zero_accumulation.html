Zeros of the ADO meridian state approach the complete point

31 October 2026.

For a knot whose Kashaev invariant has positive exponential growth, the reduced ADO meridian state takes exponentially different values at two points separated by order 1/N1/N. Its value at the complete point is the Kashaev invariant, whereas its value one quantum shift away is exactly 11. The state is a Laurent polynomial whose degree grows at most linearly with NN. Together these facts force actual zeros within order 1/N1/N of the complete point. This note proves an explicit bound on that distance, retaining the marked meridian coordinate and the full modified-dimension factor.

The obstruction to a uniform nonzero scalar asymptotic expression across the two points was already described in the formal A-curve period discussion and the regular-period construction. The quantitative zero bound below makes that obstruction precise for the actual knot state. It does not identify a classical A-polynomial factor. The canonical regular remainder, defined by subtracting the ordinary-color polar data, remains a separate candidate for such a comparison.

1. The actual state and its two nearby values

Fix a zero-framed knot KK for which the actual all-level volume limit is known. Write KN(K)K_N(K) for its reduced Kashaev invariant, with the unknot normalized to 11, and assume

qN=eπi/N,limN→∞1Nlog⁡|KN(K)|=v=V2π>0. q_N=e^{\pi i/N},\qquad \lim_{N\to\infty}\frac1N\log|K_N(K)| =v=\frac{V}{2\pi}>0. \tag{1}

For example, this applies to the fixed long-twist knots covered by the strict Conway-tree theorem and the ordered net-twist theorem. In either application, the knot and its integer twist weights are fixed before NN tends to infinity. The argument below otherwise uses no restriction on the knot presentation.

Let FN(M)F_N(M) be its reduced ADO state. It is the zero-framed cut-tangle scalar, equivalently the full modified-trace invariant divided by the modified dimension. The relation between the unrolled color parameter α\alpha, its exponential xx, and the marked meridian eigenvalue MM is

x=qNα,M=−qNx. x=q_N^\alpha,\qquad M=-q_Nx. \tag{2}

Thus α=N−1\alpha=N-1 gives M=1M=1. The exact ADO normalization and integer-color specialization give

FN(−qNj)=Cj(qN)(1≤j≤N),FN(1)=KN(K),C1(q)=1. F_N(-q_N^j)=C_j(q_N)\quad(1\le j\le N),\qquad F_N(1)=K_N(K),\qquad C_1(q)=1. \tag{3}

Here CjC_j denotes the reduced jj-dimensional ordinary quantum-group invariant of the same knot. In particular, its one-dimensional value is 11.

The squared-meridian descent proves the exact identity

FN(−M)=FN(M)(N≥2). F_N(-M)=F_N(M) \qquad(N\ge2). \tag{4}

There is no even-level exception in (4). To recall why the normalization introduces no sign, choose a bb-strand braid of writhe ww closing to KK. Its closure has one component, so the braid permutation is a bb-cycle and w≡b−1(mod⁡2)w\equiv b-1\pmod2. The integer κD=w−b+1\kappa_D=w-b+1 is therefore even. In the exact meridian-shift insertion, all crossing factors depend on Y=M2Y=M^2. Their product over the NN shifts Y↦qN2YY\mapsto q_N^2Y is 11: the numerator and denominator products cancel by ∏r=0N−1(1−qN2rz)=1−zN\prod_{r=0}^{N-1}(1-q_N^{2r}z)=1-z^N. The remaining framing factor has cyclic product qN−NκD=1q_N^{-N\kappa_D}=1. After NN shifts, MM has become −M-M, proving (4). At a vanishing numerator or denominator this is the cancelled rational identity, not a quotient of separately evaluated zeros.

Equations (3) and (4) now imply, exactly at every level,

FN(qN)=FN(−qN)=1. F_N(q_N)=F_N(-q_N)=1. \tag{5}

For sufficiently large NN, (1) also gives KN(K)≠0K_N(K)\ne0. Consequently

FN(qN)FN(1)=1KN(K),limN→∞1Nlog⁡|FN(qN)FN(1)|=−v. \frac{F_N(q_N)}{F_N(1)}=\frac1{K_N(K)},\qquad \lim_{N\to\infty}\frac1N \log\left|\frac{F_N(q_N)}{F_N(1)}\right|=-v. \tag{6}

The shift in (6) is the actual meridian shift M↦qNMM\mapsto q_NM. Writing FN(M)=fN(Y)F_N(M)=f_N(Y) and QN=qN2Q_N=q_N^2, its squared-coordinate form is Y↦QNYY\mapsto Q_NY, with fN(QN)=1f_N(Q_N)=1.

2. A two-value estimate for Laurent polynomials

The following elementary estimate explains why a degree bound matters. For a nonzero Laurent polynomial P(z)=∑j=mnajzjP(z)=\sum_{j=m}^{n}a_jz^j with aman≠0a_ma_n\ne0, call D=n−mD=n-m its exponent spread. Multiplication by a Laurent monomial changes neither this spread nor the nonzero roots.

Two-value lemma. Suppose z0,z1≠0z_0,z_1\ne0 have equal modulus, P(z0)P(z1)≠0P(z_0)P(z_1)\ne0, and

A=|P(z0)P(z1)|>1,δ=|z1−z0|. A=\left|\frac{P(z_0)}{P(z_1)}\right|>1,\qquad \delta=|z_1-z_0|. \tag{7}

Then D>0D>0, and some nonzero root of PP lies at distance at most

δ1−A−1/D \frac{\delta}{1-A^{-1/D}} \tag{8}

from z0z_0.

To prove this, factor the ordinary polynomial obtained by removing the lowest Laurent monomial:

z−mP(z)=an∏j=1D(z−ζj). z^{-m}P(z)=a_n\prod_{j=1}^{D}(z-\zeta_j). \tag{9}

Its constant coefficient is nonzero, so all ζj\zeta_j are nonzero. If D=0D=0, the equal moduli of z0z_0 and z1z_1 would give A=1A=1. For D>0D>0, put r=min⁡j|z0−ζj|r=\min_j|z_0-\zeta_j|. If r≤δr\le\delta, (8) already follows. Otherwise, the Laurent monomial has modulus ratio 11, and the triangle inequality gives

−log⁡A=∑j=1Dlog⁡|z1−ζj||z0−ζj|≥Dlog⁡(1−δr). \begin{aligned} -\log A &=\sum_{j=1}^{D}\log \frac{|z_1-\zeta_j|}{|z_0-\zeta_j|}\\ &\ge D\log\left(1-\frac{\delta}{r}\right). \end{aligned} \tag{10}

Solving (10) for rr proves (8). The proof retains root multiplicities and requires no information about their angles or simplicity. A larger upper bound for DD may replace DD in (8), giving a weaker valid bound.

3. The finite crossing matrices give a linear degree bound

Fix any braid presentation of KK with bb strands and LL crossings. Let L+L_+ and L−L_- count the positive and negative crossings, so L=L++L−L=L_++L_- and w=L+−L−w=L_+-L_-. Put d=N−1d=N-1.

The finite cut-state construction retains all indices in {0,…,d}\{0,\ldots,d\}. The explicit positive and negative crossing entries make their color dependence particularly transparent. After stripping the common Cartan scalar, the positive entry with input indices (a,b)(a,b) and shift kk is an xx-independent coefficient times

x−a−b−k(Qx2/Qa;Q)k,0≤k≤min⁡(a,d−b),Q=qN2. x^{-a-b-k}(Qx^2/Q^a;Q)_k, \qquad 0\le k\le\min(a,d-b),\qquad Q=q_N^2. \tag{11}

Here (u;Q)k=∏r=0k−1(1−uQr)(u;Q)_k=\prod_{r=0}^{k-1}(1-uQ^r). The negative entry is an xx-independent coefficient times

xa+b−k(Qx2/Qb;Q)k,0≤k≤min⁡(b,d−a). x^{a+b-k}(Qx^2/Q^b;Q)_k, \qquad 0\le k\le\min(b,d-a). \tag{12}

These are the actual checked crossings; the two shift cutoffs are different and have both been retained. Expanding the finite products in (11) and (12) gives the respective exponent intervals

[−a−b−k,−a−b+k]⊂[−2d,0],[a+b−k,a+b+k]⊂[0,2d]. \begin{aligned} [-a-b-k,-a-b+k]&\subset[-2d,0],\\ [a+b-k,a+b+k]&\subset[0,2d]. \end{aligned} \tag{13}

For example, in the positive case k≤d−bk\le d-b bounds a+b+ka+b+k by 2d2d, while k≤ak\le a bounds −a−b+k-a-b+k by 00. The negative case uses k≤d−ak\le d-a and k≤bk\le b. The factorial denominators depend only on qNq_N, are nonzero for their actual indices below NN, and introduce no color denominator.

The common Cartan scalars cancel against the zero-framing twist correction. What remains of that correction is xwdx^{wd}. Each of the b−1b-1 pivotal traces contributes x−dx^{-d} times an xx-independent factor. Thus the common remaining monomial is xd(w−b+1)x^{d(w-b+1)}. Products and finite sums cannot enlarge the exponent interval. After including this monomial, the support of the actual reduced state lies in

[−d(L+b−1),d(L−b+1)]. [-d(L+b-1),\ d(L-b+1)]. \tag{14}

The substitution x=−qN−1Mx=-q_N^{-1}M only multiplies coefficients by nonzero constants. It leaves (14) unchanged. In particular the actual Laurent spread, after any cancellations, satisfies

DN≤2L(N−1),DNY≤L(N−1). D_N\le2L(N-1),\qquad D_N^Y\le L(N-1). \tag{15}

The second bound is for fN(Y)f_N(Y): exact evenness (4) divides every actual exponent and its spread by two. These estimates hold at both parities and do not require a bound on the number of summands. For the knots in (1), L>0L>0 and both spreads are positive for sufficiently large NN, because a Laurent monomial cannot satisfy (6).

4. A quantitative zero bound at the complete point

Let

rN=min⁡{|z−1|:z≠0,FN(z)=0},δN=|qN−1|=2sin⁡π2N. r_N=\min\{|z-1|:z\ne0,\ F_N(z)=0\},\qquad \delta_N=|q_N-1|=2\sin\frac{\pi}{2N}. \tag{16}

For all sufficiently large NN, the two-value lemma applied at 11 and qNq_N gives

rN≤δN1−|KN(K)|−1/DN≤δN1−|KN(K)|−1/[2L(N−1)]. r_N\le \frac{\delta_N}{1-|K_N(K)|^{-1/D_N}} \le\frac{\delta_N} {1-|K_N(K)|^{-1/[2L(N-1)]}}. \tag{17}

In particular,

limsupN→∞NrN≤π1−exp⁡(−V/(4πL)). \limsup_{N\to\infty}Nr_N \le\frac{\pi}{1-\exp(-V/(4\pi L))}. \tag{18}

The squared-meridian coordinate improves the constant. Apply the same lemma to fNf_N at 11 and QNQ_N. Its spread is at most L(N−1)L(N-1), and |QN−1|∼2π/N|Q_N-1|\sim2\pi/N. Hence it has a zero ζN\zeta_N with

limsupN→∞N|ζN−1|≤2π1−exp⁡(−V/(2πL)). \limsup_{N\to\infty}N|\zeta_N-1| \le\frac{2\pi}{1-\exp(-V/(2\pi L))}. \tag{19}

Choose the square root zNz_N of ζN\zeta_N that tends to 11. It is a zero of FNF_N, and |zN−1|=|ζN−1|/|zN+1||z_N-1|=|\zeta_N-1|/|z_N+1|, where |zN+1|→2|z_N+1|\to2. We obtain the stronger estimate

limsupN→∞NrN≤π1−exp⁡(−V/(2πL)). \boxed{\displaystyle \limsup_{N\to\infty}Nr_N \le\frac{\pi}{1-\exp(-V/(2\pi L))}.} \tag{20}

Every fixed braid presentation supplies a valid LL. This is a bound on zeros of the actual reduced knot state, not an auxiliary polynomial chosen to have the same complete value. It does not give their number in the shrinking disk, their limiting directions, or a classical geometric interpretation of their factors.

5. Consequences for shift ratios and the full normalization

There is no fixed complex neighborhood of M=1M=1 on which all sufficiently large FNF_N are nonvanishing. Moreover, (6) directly rules out a finite nonzero limit of FN(qNM)/FN(M)F_N(q_NM)/F_N(M) at M=1M=1. These are distinct assertions: a zero of FNF_N need not be a pole of that quotient, because a shifted numerator zero may cancel it.

For example, a scalar WKB expression on a fixed neighborhood of 11 would require a holomorphic action SS and nonvanishing amplitudes aNa_N converging locally uniformly to a nonzero holomorphic function, with

FN(M)=cNeNS(M)aN(M),cN≠0. F_N(M)=c_N e^{NS(M)}a_N(M),\qquad c_N\ne0. \tag{21}

Such an expression would imply FN(qN)/FN(1)→exp⁡(πiS′(1))≠0F_N(q_N)/F_N(1)\to\exp(\pi iS'(1))\ne0, contradicting (6). It is also incompatible with (20). This does not exclude expressions on domains omitting the complete point, or with level-dependent divisors explicitly retained.

A scalar normalization independent of MM changes neither the roots nor the ratio in (6). A fixed rational or algebraic gauge, on a branch that is holomorphic and nonzero near (q,M)=(1,1)(q,M)=(1,1), also cannot remove the roots and has ratio tending to 11 between the two points. Gauges with moving poles or zeros require separate analysis.

The actual full modified-trace state is an important example of the latter case. Its exact factor is

𝒩N(M)=dN(M)FN(M),dN(M)=ηNM−M−1MN−M−N,ηN=iN+3,dN(1)=ηN/N. \mathcal N_N(M)=d_N(M)F_N(M),\qquad d_N(M)=\eta_N\frac{M-M^{-1}}{M^N-M^{-N}},\qquad \eta_N=i^{N+3},\qquad d_N(1)=\eta_N/N. \tag{22}

At M=qNM=q_N the denominator in (22) has derivative −2NqN−1-2Nq_N^{-1}. Since FN(qN)=1F_N(q_N)=1, the full state has the uncancelled simple pole

Res⁡M=qN𝒩N(M)=ηN(1−qN2)2N≠0(N≥2). \operatorname{Res}_{M=q_N}\mathcal N_N(M) =\frac{\eta_N(1-q_N^2)}{2N}\ne0 \qquad(N\ge2). \tag{23}

Its value at M=1M=1 is nevertheless the regular whole-germ value ηNKN(K)/N\eta_NK_N(K)/N. Thus the full normalization does not supply a holomorphic nonzero scalar packet across these points either. For sufficiently large NN, its shift quotient has a pole at M=1M=1, consistent with the exact normalization gauge

dN(qNM)dN(M)=−qN−1qN2M2−1M2−1. \frac{d_N(q_NM)}{d_N(M)} =-q_N^{-1}\frac{q_N^2M^2-1}{M^2-1}. \tag{24}

6. The canonical regular remainder is a different object

The regular-period construction subtracts prescribed polar data, rather than applying a nonzero scalar normalization. Let PNordP_N^{\mathrm{ord}} be the unique polynomial of degree at most N−2N-2 taking the values Cj(qN)C_j(q_N) at QNjQ_N^j for 1≤j<N1\le j<N. Define

SN(Y)=YN−1Y−1,RNreg(Y)=fN(Y)−PNord(Y)SN(Y). S_N(Y)=\frac{Y^N-1}{Y-1},\qquad R_N^{\mathrm{reg}}(Y) =\frac{f_N(Y)-P_N^{\mathrm{ord}}(Y)}{S_N(Y)}. \tag{25}

All roots of SNS_N are simple ordinary-color points. The numerator vanishes there, so RNregR_N^{\mathrm{reg}} is Laurent polynomial. In the full state this gives the exact separation

𝒩N(M)=ηNMN−1RNreg(M2)+ηNMN−1PNord(M2)SN(M2). \mathcal N_N(M) =\eta_NM^{N-1}R_N^{\mathrm{reg}}(M^2) +\eta_NM^{N-1}\frac{P_N^{\mathrm{ord}}(M^2)}{S_N(M^2)}. \tag{26}

The second term contains all finite nonzero polar principal parts. The ordinary-color bound in the same construction gives |PNord(1)/N|=OK(Nb−1)|P_N^{\mathrm{ord}}(1)/N|=O_K(N^{b-1}). Therefore

RNreg(1)=KN(K)−PNord(1)N,limN→∞1Nlog⁡|RNreg(1)|=v. R_N^{\mathrm{reg}}(1) =\frac{K_N(K)-P_N^{\mathrm{ord}}(1)}{N},\qquad \lim_{N\to\infty}\frac1N\log|R_N^{\mathrm{reg}}(1)|=v. \tag{27}

Its first shifted value is not the ordinary anchor 11. It is instead

RNreg(QN)=fN′(QN)−(PNord)′(QN)SN′(QN). R_N^{\mathrm{reg}}(Q_N) =\frac{f_N'(Q_N)-(P_N^{\mathrm{ord}})'(Q_N)}{S_N'(Q_N)}. \tag{28}

Thus the two-value obstruction proved for FNF_N does not automatically apply to RNregR_N^{\mathrm{reg}}. Nor does (27) prove a neighborhood asymptotic or a shift-ratio limit for this remainder.

A specified actual all-level annihilator gives the existing affine equation for the regular remainder, after its fixed rational gauge and denominator clearing. Its inhomogeneous term has fixed Laurent support and polynomially bounded coefficients. One possible next theorem would provide a locally uniform, nonzero shift-ratio limit for this actual regular state together with a locally uniform exponential lower bound. On a domain avoiding the fixed coefficient poles, such bounds would make the inhomogeneous term negligible after division by the state. The limiting ratio would then satisfy the homogeneous classical symbol. Its identification with the preferred geometric longitude would still require the marked geometric comparison.

The complete scalar volume theorem and the pointed quantum A-period and Gram formulas remain valid. What changes is the proposed neighborhood statement: the reduced state has the exponential one-step drop and nearby zeros proved above, and the full state has a nearby pole. The canonical regular remainder must be studied with its own affine equation and its ordinary-color subtraction intact.

7. The boundary factor of every polynomial annihilator

The same ordinary-color bounds give an exact algebraic restriction on the recurrence. The squared-meridian version is the necessity theorem in the peripheral normal-period note. For the positive-volume knots in (1), its hypothesis is now established. Here is the corresponding statement without requiring an even operator.

Let

P=∑i=0dai(q,M)Ti,TM=qMT,ai∈ℂ[q±1,M±1] P=\sum_{i=0}^{d}a_i(q,M)T^i,\qquad TM=qMT,\qquad a_i\in\mathbb C[q^{\pm1},M^{\pm1}] \tag{29}

be any fixed genuine all-level annihilator of the reduced state, after clearing its rational denominators. Then

(q2iM2−1)∣ai(q,M)(0≤i≤d),P=B(M2−1) (q^{2i}M^2-1)\mid a_i(q,M)\quad(0\le i\le d),\qquad P=B(M^2-1) \tag{30}

for a polynomial quantum-torus operator BB. This conclusion concerns all annihilators in the specified integral ring, not just a chosen minimal generator.

Fix ii and evaluate the exact annihilation equation at M=±qN−iM=\pm q_N^{-i}, with N>dN>d. The iith term contains FN(±1)=KN(K)F_N(\pm1)=K_N(K). Every other term is an ordinary-color value. If k=j−i>0k=j-i>0, evenness and (3) give FN(±qNk)=Ck(qN)F_N(\pm q_N^k)=C_k(q_N). If k<0k<0, use qNN=−1q_N^N=-1 to obtain FN(±qNk)=CN+k(qN)F_N(\pm q_N^k)=C_{N+k}(q_N). All these indices lie between 11 and N−1N-1. The fixed-braid bound is uniform over that entire range: |Cr(qN)|≤(N/2)b−1|C_r(q_N)|\le(N/2)^{b-1}.

The fixed Laurent coefficients in (29) are bounded on these unit-circle trajectories. Hence the annihilation equation gives

|ai(qN,±qN−i)||KN(K)|≤CPNb−1. |a_i(q_N,\pm q_N^{-i})|\,|K_N(K)|\le C_PN^{b-1}. \tag{31}

If the Laurent polynomial ai(q,±q−i)a_i(q,\pm q^{-i}) were nonzero, its finite vanishing order ν\nu at q=1q=1 would give a lower bound cN−νcN^{-\nu} at qNq_N for all sufficiently large NN. Equation (31) would then bound |KN(K)||K_N(K)| by a polynomial, contradicting (1). Thus both trajectory polynomials vanish identically. Their kernels are the relatively prime ideals generated by M−q−iM-q^{-i} and M+q−iM+q^{-i}, proving the divisibility in (30). Finally, Ti(M2−1)=(q2iM2−1)TiT^i(M^2-1)=(q^{2i}M^2-1)T^i, which proves the displayed order of multiplication. The finite exceptional levels of the annihilator do not affect this argument.

For a polynomial annihilator in the squared-coordinate ring Y=M2Y=M^2, with shift 𝖲Y=q2Y𝖲\mathsf S Y=q^2Y\mathsf S, the statement is PY=BY(Y−1)P_Y=B_Y(Y-1). An arbitrary unsquared operator need not itself have even coefficients. It splits as P0(q,Y,𝖲)+MP1(q,Y,𝖲)P_0(q,Y,\mathsf S)+M P_1(q,Y,\mathsf S); evenness of the state makes the two parts separate annihilators, and the argument applies to each. Thus the squared polynomial annihilator ideal is contained in the left ideal generated by Y−1Y-1. Its cyclic module has the boundary quotient

Δ=ℛ/ℛ(Y−1),ℛ=ℂ(q)[Y±1,𝖲±1]. \Delta=\mathcal R/\mathcal R(Y-1),\qquad \mathcal R=\mathbb C(q)[Y^{\pm1},\mathsf S^{\pm1}]. \tag{32}

The commutation rule above is understood in this ring. Localizing to ℂ(q,Y)\mathbb C(q,Y) makes Y−1Y-1 invertible and kills this quotient. The boundary information must therefore be retained in a meridian lattice alongside the localized recurrence. This is a normalization factor of shift order zero; it is not the sought geometric factor involving the longitude variable.

One must also keep the change of state when removing the factor: BB annihilates (M2−1)FN(M)(M^2-1)F_N(M), whose value at completeness is zero. The knot invariant is its normal derivative,

KN(K)=12ddlog⁡M[(M2−1)FN(M)]|M=1. K_N(K)=\left.\frac12\frac{d}{d\log M} \bigl[(M^2-1)F_N(M)\bigr]\right|_{M=1}. \tag{33}

The peripheral normal-period note develops the corresponding affine lifting equation and states the additional rank condition under which its first normal derivative determines the period. Neither (30) nor the nearby-zero bound proves that rank condition or identifies the remaining classical characteristic factor with the geometric longitude. They specify boundary data that such a comparison must retain.
