An absolute source sum can exceed volume while its signed sum cancels

The finite source of a marked diagram is a sum over shared face degrees. Taking the absolute value of each summand loses information: in the example below, the resulting positive sum grows faster than the complete augmented volume rate, at every sufficiently large level of either parity. At the same spectral tuple, the signed sum has a strictly smaller rate along odd levels.

Both statements concern one genuine source sector of one unchanged marked diagram. The selected crossing uses its left recurrence-path piece, and every other crossing uses its central piece. No maximum over different spectator sectors is used. The spectral value is +2+2 at the selected crossing and −2-2 at every spectator.

The proof first turns a positive, target-balanced triangle table into actual integer source arrays. It retains the different finite degrees at even and odd levels. It then combines the known local coefficient entropy with the exact rational certificate for the 54-region example. The final cancellation statement also uses the separately proved odd-level signed cap bound. No estimate for a filled period or knot invariant is inferred from an absolute-array lower bound.

5. Rounding the table without changing a target

We first record the finite rounding argument used at both parities. Suppose a supported nonnegative real table has integer row and column sums. Subtract its entrywise floors. The remaining fractional table has integer margins. In its bipartite row-column graph, alter entries alternately around a cycle until an entry becomes zero or one, always staying in [0,1][0,1]. This preserves all margins and reduces the number of fractional entries. A nonempty forest of fractional entries is impossible: a leaf would have an integer residual margin and exactly one fractional entry. Repetition therefore produces an integer table within the floor and ceiling of every original entry.

Before this rounding, a bounded correction to the column sums can be made without changing row sums. Choose a spanning tree of the connected ordinary spectator graph, and for each tree edge choose a spectator triangle containing its two endpoints. Move the required column imbalances along the tree, by subtracting at one corner and adding at the other corner of that same row. A fixed bounded imbalance changes every entry by a bounded amount. Since all αtf\alpha_{tf} are positive on the fixed support, the corrected tables below remain nonnegative for all sufficiently large NN.

7. Every positive diagonal Cauchy reference has the same limitation

For any finite complex array (Aa)a∈I(A_a)_{a\in I}, infwa>0(∑awa)1/2(∑a|Aa|2wa)1/2=∑a|Aa|.(32) \inf_{w_a>0} \left(\sum_a w_a\right)^{1/2} \left(\sum_a\frac{|A_a|^2}{w_a}\right)^{1/2} =\sum_a|A_a|. \qquad\text{(32)} Cauchy’s inequality gives the lower bound. If all entries vanish, both sides are zero.

Apply (32) to the original array partition in (3). This also holds for level-dependent gauges.

This limitation concerns diagonal positive references on the unchanged array partition. A non-diagonal Hermitian form or a regrouped sum can retain phase correlations before taking absolute values. Equation (32) does not rule out those estimates.

10. Finite verification and proof scope

The source checker independently evaluates the original polynomial matrix VN(σ)V_N(\sigma) over finite fields, checks its protected coefficients and odd central factorization, and constructs exact rounded tables with the original targets. Those are finite checks of the identities and incidences. The all-level lower bound uses the written rounding argument and the uniform local coefficient theorem (17), not an extrapolation from the checked levels.

The complete-volume comparison (30) uses the separate marked geometric construction and its rational action certificate. The signed upper bound (34) uses the separate stable-exterior theorem, its pressure certificates and omitted-tail gap. These inputs have different purposes; none follows merely from the finite-field checks or the absolute-array construction.
