The original wire saddle stays in its factorial chamber on every positive path

Research proof, 3 November 2026. This uses the coupled original period equations, not merely separate bounds on the endpoint angles. It proves the real wire order throughout the positive path charge cube for every integer power at least three. The conclusion places the geometric critical points in the original regular factorial chamber. It does not assert that the geometric saddle dominates the full finite kernel or that its coefficient in a general source is nonzero.

1. Inputs and a cosine maximum principle

Take the finite positive paths of the fractional-charge theorem: selected powers nv≥3n_v\ge3, charges 0≤χv≤10\le\chi_v\le1, original incidence degrees at most two, and the stated complete outside endpoints. At an active vertex write tv=ϑv−iHv,0<ϑv<π,Hv>0.(1) t_v=\vartheta_v-iH_v,\qquad 0<\vartheta_v<\pi,\quad H_v>0. \qquad\text{(1)} At zero-charge and complete vertices, tv=0t_v=0. The original return and both period equations are rvu=kvu+igvu=asinh⁡(tan⁡(tv/2)cos⁡(tu/2)),∑u∼vkvu=nvHv2,∑u∼vgvu=nvϑv2−πχv.(2) \begin{split} r_{vu}&=k_{vu}+ig_{vu} =\operatorname{asinh}\bigl(\tan(t_v/2)\cos(t_u/2)\bigr),\\ \sum_{u\sim v}k_{vu}&=\frac{n_vH_v}{2},& \sum_{u\sim v}g_{vu}&=\frac{n_v\vartheta_v}{2}-\pi\chi_v . \end{split} \qquad\text{(2)} At an active endpoint, kvu>0k_{vu}>0 and −π/2<gvu<π/2-\pi/2<g_{vu}<\pi/2. The cosine-phase maximum principle supplies βv:=arg⁡cos⁡(tv/2)∈(0,π/4),βv=0at a complete vertex.(3) \beta_v:=\arg\cos(t_v/2)\in(0,\pi/4), \qquad \beta_v=0\ \text{at a complete vertex}. \qquad\text{(3)} Only the strict qualitative bound in (3) is needed below.

Lemma 1. Every active vertex satisfies |cos⁡(tv/2)|<1.(4) |\cos(t_v/2)|<1. \qquad\text{(4)}

Proof. If (4) failed, choose an active vertex at which C=|cos⁡(tv/2)|≥1C=|\cos(t_v/2)|\ge1 is maximal over all selected and complete vertices. Every neighbor then has |cos⁡(tu/2)|≤C|\cos(t_u/2)|\le C, so |sinh⁡rvu|=|tan⁡(tv/2)cos⁡(tu/2)|≤|sin⁡(tv/2)|.(5) |\sinh r_{vu}| =|\tan(t_v/2)\cos(t_u/2)| \le|\sin(t_v/2)|. \qquad\text{(5)} Since |sinh⁡(k+ig)|2=sinh⁡2k+sin⁡2g|\sinh(k+ig)|^2=\sinh^2k+\sin^2g, this gives kvu≤asinh⁡|sin⁡(tv/2)|k_{vu}\le\operatorname{asinh}|\sin(t_v/2)|. Put tv=ϑ−iHt_v=\vartheta-iH. The supposition C≥1C\ge1 implies cosh⁡H+cos⁡ϑ≥2,|sin⁡(tv/2)|2=cosh⁡H−cos⁡ϑ2≤2sinh⁡2(H/2).(6) \cosh H+\cos\vartheta\ge2,\qquad |\sin(t_v/2)|^2=\frac{\cosh H-\cos\vartheta}{2} \le2\sinh^2(H/2). \qquad\text{(6)} But for every H>0H>0, asinh⁡(2sinh⁡(H/2))<3H4.(7) \operatorname{asinh}\bigl(\sqrt2\sinh(H/2)\bigr)<\frac{3H}{4}. \qquad\text{(7)} Indeed sinh⁡(3H/4)>(3/2)sinh⁡(H/2)>2sinh⁡(H/2)\sinh(3H/4)>(3/2)\sinh(H/2)> \sqrt2\sinh(H/2); the first inequality follows from the strict increase of sinh⁡x/x\sinh x/x on x>0x>0. This maximum principle itself uses neither the quantitative height bound nor the charges’ upper bound. It uses the original real period equation, degree at most two, and the positivity of the real returns. At complete vertices the cosine modulus is exactly one.

2. The selected wire lies in the upper quarter strip

For an edge vuvu, put A=cos⁡2(tv/2),B=cos⁡2(tu/2),k=ABA+B,x=1πarcsin⁡k.(8) A=\cos^2(t_v/2),\qquad B=\cos^2(t_u/2),\qquad k=\frac{AB}{A+B},\qquad x=\frac1\pi\arcsin\sqrt{k}. \qquad\text{(8)} These are the selected branches continued from the complete edge, where A=B=1A=B=1 and x=1/4x=1/4. By (3), A,BA,B have positive real part and nonnegative imaginary part. By Lemma 1 their moduli are at most one, strictly less than one at an active endpoint. For an edge with an active endpoint, kk has positive real and positive imaginary parts, so the branch in (8) has 0<ℜx<1/20<\Re x<1/2 and ℑx>0\Im x>0.

Lemma 2. Every edge with an active endpoint satisfies 0<ℜk<12,0<ℜx<14,ℜx>α(ℑx),α(y)=arccos⁡(sech⁡(2πy))2π.(9) 0<\Re k<\frac12,\qquad 0<\Re x<\frac14,\qquad \Re x>\alpha(\Im x),\quad \alpha(y)=\frac{\arccos(\operatorname{sech}(2\pi y))}{2\pi}. \qquad\text{(9)}

Proof. Write 1/A=re−iα,1/B=se−iβ,r,s≥1,0≤α,β<π/2.(10) 1/A=r e^{-i\alpha},\qquad 1/B=s e^{-i\beta}, \quad r,s\ge1,\quad 0\le\alpha,\beta<\pi/2. \qquad\text{(10)} The function |re−iα+se−iβ−1|2|r e^{-i\alpha}+s e^{-i\beta}-1|^2 is strictly increasing in either radius for r,s≥1r,s\ge1: half its rr-derivative is r+scos⁡(α−β)−cos⁡α>0r+s\cos(\alpha-\beta)-\cos\alpha>0, and similarly for ss. At r=s=1r=s=1, subtracting one from this function gives 2[(1−cos⁡α)(1−cos⁡β)+sin⁡αsin⁡β]≥0.(11) 2\bigl[(1-\cos\alpha)(1-\cos\beta)+\sin\alpha\sin\beta\bigr]\ge0. \qquad\text{(11)} At least one radius is strictly greater than one. Thus, with Z=1/A+1/BZ=1/A+1/B, one has |Z−1|>1|Z-1|>1. Equivalently |Z|2>2ℜZ|Z|^2>2\Re Z, so ℜk=ℜ(1/Z)<1/2\Re k=\Re(1/Z)<1/2. Its positivity was already noted after (8).

Now cos⁡(2πx)=1−2k\cos(2\pi x)=1-2k. Taking real parts shows 0<cos⁡(2πℜx)cosh⁡(2πℑx)<1.(12) 0<\cos(2\pi\Re x)\cosh(2\pi\Im x)<1. \qquad\text{(12)} The first inequality implies ℜx<1/4\Re x<1/4; the second is exactly the last inequality in (9). ▫\square

For an all-complete edge, x=1/4x=1/4; it is a constant part of the geometric configuration. The selected square root q=cot⁡(πx),q2=1+tan⁡2(tv/2)+tan⁡2(tu/2)(13) q=\cot(\pi x),\qquad q^2=1+\tan^2(t_v/2)+\tan^2(t_u/2) \qquad\text{(13)} is the original one. Equation (13) follows from (8) and is continued from q=1q=1.

3. The coupled equations force the real wire order

Theorem 3. On every edge of the original positive path, throughout its whole charge cube, ℜx<min⁡{12−ℜtv2π,12−ℜtu2π}.(14) \boxed{\quad \Re x< \min\left\{\frac12-\frac{\Re t_v}{2\pi}, \frac12-\frac{\Re t_u}{2\pi}\right\}. \quad} \qquad\text{(14)}

Proof. It suffices to prove the inequality at one endpoint vv. It is automatic when that endpoint is complete. Suppose it fails at an active endpoint. Use radian coordinates T=tv/2=a−ih,W=πx=A0+iY,A0≥π/2−a.(15) T=t_v/2=a-ih,\qquad W=\pi x=A_0+iY, \qquad A_0\ge\pi/2-a . \qquad\text{(15)} Here 0<a<π/20<a<\pi/2, h>0h>0, and Lemma 2 gives 0<A0<π/4,Y>00<A_0<\pi/4,\ Y>0. In particular a>π/4a>\pi/4.

The upper-right-quadrant property gives ℜ(AB‾)>0\Re(A\bar B)>0, hence |A+B|>|B||A+B|>|B|, in the notation of (8). Consequently |sin⁡2W|=|k|<|A|=|cos⁡2T|.(16) |\sin^2W|=|k|<|A|=|\cos^2T|. \qquad\text{(16)} In (16) the two sides are sin⁡2A0+sinh⁡2Y\sin^2A_0+\sinh^2Y and cos⁡2a+sinh⁡2h\cos^2a+\sinh^2h. Since A0≥π/2−aA_0\ge\pi/2-a, their real trigonometric terms have the opposite weak order. It follows that Y<hY<h.

The original return satisfies the exact identity e2rvu=q+tan⁡Tq−tan⁡T=cos⁡(W−T)cos⁡(W+T).(17) e^{2r_{vu}} =\frac{q+\tan T}{q-\tan T} =\frac{\cos(W-T)}{\cos(W+T)}. \qquad\text{(17)} For the first equality use sinh⁡rvu=tan⁡Tcos⁡(tu/2)\sinh r_{vu}=\tan T\cos(t_u/2) and cosh⁡rvu=qcos⁡(tu/2)\cosh r_{vu}=q\cos(t_u/2). The latter square-root sign is fixed at completeness and continues on the positive charge cube; neither side vanishes there. The second equality uses q=cot⁡Wq=\cot W. Thus (17) retains the original return.

In (15), 0<a−A0<π/20<a-A_0<\pi/2 and Y+h>0Y+h>0, so 0<arg⁡cos⁡(W−T)=arctan⁡(tan⁡(a−A0)tanh⁡(Y+h))<a−A0.(18) 0<\arg\cos(W-T) =\arctan\bigl(\tan(a-A_0)\tanh(Y+h)\bigr) <a-A_0 . \qquad\text{(18)} Also π/2≤A0+a<3π/4\pi/2\le A_0+a<3\pi/4 and Y−h<0Y-h<0. The denominator in (17) therefore has nonpositive real and strictly positive imaginary parts: π/2≤arg⁡cos⁡(W+T)<π.(19) \pi/2\le\arg\cos(W+T)<\pi . \qquad\text{(19)} The argument of their quotient belongs to (−π,0)(-\pi,0). Since the original return has 2gvu∈(−π,π)2g_{vu}\in(-\pi,\pi), there is no additional 2π2\pi ambiguity in (17). Equations (18)–(19) yield the strict estimate gvu<a−A0−π/22≤a−π/2<0.(20) g_{vu}<\frac{a-A_0-\pi/2}{2} \le a-\pi/2<0. \qquad\text{(20)}

Put Bv=sinh⁡Hv/sin⁡ϑvB_v=\sinh H_v/\sin\vartheta_v and ω=arctan⁡Bv\omega=\arctan B_v. The argument of tan⁡Tcos⁡(tu/2)\tan T\cos(t_u/2) is βu−ω\beta_u-\omega. Its real part is positive, since it equals sinh⁡kvucos⁡gvu\sinh k_{vu}\cos g_{vu}. When its argument is negative, the exact relation tan⁡gvu=tanh⁡kvutan⁡(βu−ω)(21) \tan g_{vu} =\tanh k_{vu}\tan(\beta_u-\omega) \qquad\text{(21)} shows that gvu>βu−ω≥−ωg_{vu}>\beta_u-\omega\ge-\omega. Thus (20) implies ω>π/2−a.(22) \omega>\pi/2-a . \qquad\text{(22)}

There can be only one further incidence at vv. Its return imaginary part, or zero if the incidence is absent, must satisfy the original period equation. By (20), gother=nva−πχv−gvu>(nv−1)a−π/2≥2a−π/2>0.(23) g_{\mathrm{ other}} =n_va-\pi\chi_v-g_{vu} >(n_v-1)a-\pi/2 \ge2a-\pi/2>0. \qquad\text{(23)} A terminal vertex is already impossible. Otherwise let ww be the other neighbor. The positive version of (21) gives 0<gother<βw−ω.(24) 0<g_{\mathrm{ other}}<\beta_w-\omega . \qquad\text{(24)} Combining (22)–(24) forces βw>2a−π/2+ω>a>π/4,(25) \beta_w>2a-\pi/2+\omega>a>\pi/4, \qquad\text{(25)} contradicting (3). This excludes the failure in (15) and proves (14) at both endpoints. ▫\square

Only nv≥3,χv≤1n_v\ge3,\ \chi_v\le1 and the original degree bound were used in (23). In particular the contradiction does not replace the actual charge by one or alter an attaching degree.

4. What this proves about the original phase

For an active edge put sv=1/2−tv/(2π)=bv+iSvs_v=1/2-t_v/(2\pi)=b_v+iS_v, and similarly at uu. Equations (9), (14), and the geometric spectral half-plane give Y,Sv,Su≥0,α(Y)<ℜx<bv,bu,α(Sv)<bv,α(Su)<bu.(26) Y,S_v,S_u\ge0,\qquad \alpha(Y)<\Re x<b_v,b_u,\qquad \alpha(S_v)<b_v,\quad\alpha(S_u)<b_u. \qquad\text{(26)} For the last two inequalities use cos⁡(2πbv)cosh⁡(2πSv)=ℜ(−2cos⁡tv)/2<1\cos(2\pi b_v)\cosh(2\pi S_v)=\Re(-2\cos t_v)/2<1. All original factorial arguments x,sv−x,1−sv−x(27) x,\quad s_v-x,\quad1-s_v-x \qquad\text{(27)} and their uu-counterparts have real parts strictly between zero and one. Thus the selected wire branch stays in the original regular factorial chart along the entire charge cube. The zero logarithmic lift of its stationary equation, fixed at completeness, continues in this chart.

For fixed endpoint angles the real part of the principal wire phase b(x,tv)+b(x,tu)b(x,t_v)+b(x,t_u), on its fixed-height line, is strictly concave on α(Y)<ℜx<min⁡(bv,bu)\alpha(Y)<\Re x<\min(b_v,b_u). Indeed, with the quantities in the mixed-phase calculation, each incidence has v>0,w>0,u<wv>0,w>0,u<w. Its negative xx-second derivative is π(v+2w−u)>0\pi(v+2w-u)>0. The geometric critical wire is therefore the unique maximum on this central interval, with a positive curvature bound on compact subintervals.

For an internal two-vertex piece with complete outer ends, the complete-end-compensated mixed phase consequently has its actual positive-charge critical point inside the concavity domain of Proposition 1 in the linked note, without requiring any height ordering. This gives strict horizontal concavity of that mixed phase on its stated convex domain. Zero-charge faces are understood by continuity and the direct nondegenerate Hessian at completeness. A general interior vertex has no extra complete-end term; this conclusion does not insert one there.

The earlier exact A=1,B=3/10+3i/5A=1,B=3/10+3i/5 wire-order counterexample obeys the separate endpoint bounds but was not an integer-filled path. Theorem 3 explains precisely why the coupled equations exclude such a failure on the actual family.

Finally, regularity and a strict central maximum do not control the rest of the finite source. The all-three bare-kernel obstruction still exhibits larger endpoint sectors before the original trace contracts them. No assertion about those sectors, global mixed currents, or the nonzero full-source saddle coefficient follows solely from (14) or (26).
