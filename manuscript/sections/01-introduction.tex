\chapter{The knots, the theorems, and their proofs}\label{ch:introduction}

\section{What the theorem compares}
The volume conjecture asks whether the growth of a sequence of finite knot invariants recovers hyperbolic volume. For each integer $N\ge2$, set $q_N=e^{\pi\ii/N}$. Write $\langle K\rangle_N$ for the reduced Kashaev invariant of a knot $K$ at this level, normalized to equal one on the unknot. For a hyperbolic knot, the complement carries a complete finite-volume hyperbolic metric. The proposed comparison is
\[
 \frac{2\pi}{N}\log|\langle K\rangle_N|
 \longrightarrow \Vol(S^3\setminus K).
\]
Chapter~\ref{ch:source} specifies its crossing matrices, traces and framing convention. Kashaev constructed the triangulated-link invariant in \cite{Kashaev1994}, gave its $R$-matrix formulation in \cite{Kashaev1995}, and proposed the volume comparison in \cite{Kashaev1997}. Its relation with the root-of-unity colored Jones polynomial is described in \cite{MurakamiMurakami2001}.

The first theorem proves this limit for the recursive family of knot diagrams constructed below, once every designated twist region has sufficiently many twists. The family permits arbitrary finite branching and nesting, subject to its precise construction rule. Chapters~\ref{ch:quantitative-circle-trees}, \ref{ch:local-circle-messages} and~\ref{ch:directed-circle-messages} treat ordered trees with one crossing-circle operator at every junction. They give explicit sufficient conditions involving degrees and signed crossing powers, both summed over vertices and imposed locally. The strongest simple local bound allows each vertex to have either sufficiently large degree or sufficiently many twists. We first explain the geometric operations and then state the construction scopes. Every construction records an actual diagram, including the order of its pieces and the positions where they are joined.

All these theorems take the same quantum limit. Choose a finite construction and signed integer twist numbers satisfying the applicable hypothesis, and hold them fixed. Then let $N$ tend to infinity. Neither the diagram nor its twist numbers grow with $N$. In the first theorem the individual twists can have different signs and arbitrarily different large magnitudes. In the quantitative theorems their sizes and junction degrees enter the sum in Theorem~\ref{thm:circle-quantitative}, the maximum in Theorem~\ref{thm:local-circle-volume}, or the directed conditions in Theorem~\ref{thm:directed-degree-twist}.

\section{A planar construction beyond tangle trees}
Chapter~\ref{ch:balanced-terminal-fans} starts from a finite simple triangulation of the sphere with at least four vertices. Pair adjacent triangles so that every triangle belongs to exactly one pair; equivalently choose a perfect matching in the cubic dual graph. The paired triangles specify crossing-circle boxes, and the remaining edges specify their strand connections. This constructs an augmented link in the three-sphere with its actual disk framings. The proof also gives a half-twist pattern for which the main strands form a knot.

The construction adds an even number of terminal tangles on each side of each original crossing circle. Every added tangle has odd crossing parity. The required numbers are chosen by an explicit recurrence in the complete packing curvatures: the curvature of a finite packing circle is the reciprocal of its radius, while the two protected lines have curvature zero. After these finitely many additions, all original and added twist regions may be filled independently with sufficiently long signed powers. The resulting knot is hyperbolic and has the volume limit above. The threshold depends on the fixed decorated diagram. No tree decomposition, common sign, or comparison of twist magnitudes is required.

The added tangles preserve the chosen endpoint pairing but generally change the knot type. Thus this result constructs new covered knots from arbitrary cores in the stated combinatorial class; it does not assert the equality for the unchanged core. Its proof controls the full finite coefficient and all Fourier charges. Appendix~\ref{app:balanced-terminal-fans} includes the circle-packing construction, the exact source after insertion, the nonzero leading contribution, and the primitive metric completion. The earlier chapters develop the tree methods and their explicit bounds separately.

\section{When no added tangles are needed}
The decoration theorem constructs diagrams satisfying a useful packing
condition. Appendix~\ref{app:weak-balance} instead starts with the
unchanged matched sphere and assumes that condition in its non-strict
form. At each matching edge, the sum of the two adjacent triangle
potentials must be at least the logarithm of either positive opposite
curvature. The appendix defines the potential by a maximization over
three nonnegative angles, gives its explicit derivatives, and states
the inequalities before proving the volume conclusion.

Equality in these inequalities is allowed. To handle it, the proof
changes the positive weights used in Cauchy's coefficient estimate
from $w_f$ to $w_f^{1-t}$, for a small fixed $t>0$. The protected
weights remain zero. The loss in every noncentral contribution is
linear in $t$, with a positive coefficient given by a triangle entropy.
The complete action is stationary, so its increase is at most a
constant times $t^2$. A sufficiently small $t$ therefore produces a
strict exponential gap for the entire complementary polynomial.
The finite filling estimate and the geometric-contribution theorem
then give the full invariant and the volume of the original knot
complement.

This is a sufficient condition on an unchanged diagram of arbitrary
finite size. The proof neither assumes a tree decomposition nor claims
that every packing satisfies the condition. In particular, the theorem
must be used with its packing hypothesis; the general
geometric-contribution theorem by itself has the smaller conclusion
recorded in the table above.

\section{Joining diagrams whose source estimates are known}

Appendix~\ref{app:protected-triangular-sums} gives a different way to
construct covered diagrams. Each input has a strict shared-weight
certificate for every locally allowed noncentral source sector. Remove
one triangle incident to its protected edge from each of two input
spheres, identify the triangular boundaries with opposite orientations,
and pair the two former partners. The result is another matched sphere.
The proof preserves the source gap by comparing the three local sectors
and accounting for the single ordinary target at the joining face.

Any fixed finite number of such pieces may be joined. Their own
knot-producing half-twist pattern and sufficiently long fixed signed
fillings give the volume limit for the assembled knot complement.
A worked sequence starts from a seventeen-region rational certificate
and attaches tetrahedral pieces. After $r$ attachments it has $17+r$
crossing regions and still fails weak balance at the retained mark.
The coefficient estimates nevertheless remain strict. This is a
construction from certified inputs, not an assertion that every
possible tangle composition has such a certificate.

Appendix~\ref{app:triangular-boundary-insertion} permits attachment at
an ordinary triangular face, away from the protected edge. Write
$(u,v,b)$ for its three positive circle curvatures. A certified piece
can be inserted when $u/b$ and $v/b$ are sufficiently small at the
complete core vector and at each coefficient witness used by the core.
The proof first minimizes the inserted disk's action with these three
weights fixed. The result is the old triangle potential plus the
piece's protected complete action. For the remaining terms, a
comparison using the entire inserted piece preserves the strict gap
and accounts for each old boundary target once.

Even chains inside an octahedral face have an explicit curvature
recurrence making both boundary ratios tend to zero. They provide
cores for any prescribed finite list of certified pieces. All final
crossing powers are then chosen sufficiently long and held fixed
before taking the quantum limit. This construction includes marked
diagrams with a balance failure which are not nontrivial protected
triangular sums at their recorded mark. It does not classify all
presentations of the resulting knots.

\section{Building the diagrams from four-ended tangles}\label{sec:geometric-construction}
A \emph{two-string tangle} is a pair of arcs in a ball whose four endpoints are fixed on its boundary. We draw its ball as a box, with two endpoints at the bottom and two at the top. Closed curves may also be present inside a box. In the augmented diagrams used in the proof, some of those curves are distinguished auxiliary circles. Composition can create further closed main components, so the eventual requirement that the filled diagram be a knot must be checked rather than assumed.

Label the boundary points in their cyclic order by
\[
 0=\text{bottom left},\quad 1=\text{bottom right},\quad
 2=\text{top right},\quad 3=\text{top left}.
\]
We use three operations, all performed on the drawn tangles with these boundary positions retained.

\paragraph{Vertical stacking.}
Place a box $F$ below a box $G$. Join the two top endpoints of $F$ to the two bottom endpoints of $G$ by short, uncrossed arcs: $F_2$ joins $G_1$ and $F_3$ joins $G_0$. Denote the resulting box by $FG$. Its exposed endpoints, in the order just defined, are $(F_0,F_1,G_2,G_3)$. Changing parentheses only changes the heights of boxes; it does not interchange their order.

\paragraph{Quarter-turn.}
Rotate the entire box clockwise through $90$ degrees in the diagram plane, carrying every strand and auxiliary circle with it. Write $\Turn F$ for the result. In terms of the old endpoints, its new ordered boundary is $(F_1,F_2,F_3,F_0)$. The algebraic evaluation implements this operation with the ordinary cup and cap matrices. It is not an arbitrary permutation of matrix entries.

\paragraph{Cylindrical closure.}
Join the exposed bottom-left endpoint to the top-left endpoint outside the box, and do the same on the right, without adding crossings. Write $C(F)$ for this closed diagram. Thus the closing pairs are $0$--$3$ and $1$--$2$. In $C(FG)$ the two connections between $F$ and $G$ and the two exterior return arcs are all part of the definition. This closure differs from joining the two top endpoints together and the two bottom endpoints together, which we call a \emph{cap closure}.

The elementary augmented box has two through strands and a small auxiliary circle surrounding them. The circle bounds a disk pierced once by each strand; this disk fixes the framing used for surgery. Denote the box by $L$, and denote its quarter-turn by $h=\Turn L$. Every occurrence of $h$ in a construction is a separate copy with its own auxiliary circle. We call such an occurrence an \emph{elementary piece}; later, when discussing its position in a construction tree, we also call it a \emph{leaf}.

To obtain the final knot, replace each auxiliary circle by twists of the strands passing through its disk. Let $R$ be one positive half twist of the two through strands; $R^{-1}$ is the negative crossing. An integer power $R^n$ is a stack of $|n|$ such crossings with the indicated sign. At elementary piece $\ell$ we retain $\eps_\ell\in\{0,1,-1\}$ half twists and perform the disk-framed integral filling with parameter $p_\ell$. The filled piece is exactly
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rational-leaf}
              \Turn\bigl(R^{\eps_\ell-2p_\ell}\bigr).
\end{equation}
A full twist contains two crossings, which accounts for the factor two. This formula fixes the sign convention for $p_\ell$ throughout the proof. After every auxiliary circle is filled, the remaining closed diagram has no auxiliary components. The main component is never filled; its complement is the manifold whose volume occurs in the theorem.

\section{Which compositions are included}\label{sec:construction-class}
Start by stacking two elementary pieces. From an already constructed box, one may take a quarter-turn, stack it with another already constructed box, or attach one new elementary piece at its bottom or top. Repeat these operations any finite number of times. We call a box obtained in this way \emph{completed}. This is a name for the construction rule, not an assertion that a hyperbolic metric has already been constructed. In particular, a single elementary piece is not completed.

\begin{definition}[The recursive construction class]\label{def:grammar}
The symbol $E$ ranges over the completed boxes just described. Their concise recursive description is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:grammar}
 E ::= hh\ \mid\ \Turn(E)\ \mid\ E_1E_2\ \mid\ hE\ \mid\ Eh.
\end{equation}
Here $::=$ means ``can be built by one of the following operations,'' and the vertical bars separate the alternatives. Each written $h$ denotes a distinct elementary piece. A closed diagram in the main theorem is $C(FG)$ with both $F$ and $G$ completed. Retain the original order of all endpoints, the crossing-disk framings, and one marked point on the main component for evaluating the reduced invariant.
\end{definition}

For example, $C((hh)(hh))$ uses four elementary pieces, with two in each of the final boxes. A longer allowed construction is $C((h\Turn(hh))(hh))$: first build the two-piece box $hh$, turn it, attach one elementary piece, and close it against another two-piece box. Repeating $E_{r+1}=h\Turn(E_r)$ from $E_0=hh$ gives arbitrarily long nested constructions. Branching is equally allowed, since any two completed boxes can be stacked. Starting instead with any stack $h^s$, $s\ge2$, gives the same class.

The last condition in Definition~\ref{def:grammar} is important: both boxes joined by the final cylindrical closure must be completed. The proof uses it to place every stationary leaf parameter strictly above $1/4$, as shown in Lemma~\ref{lem:port-bounds}. Chapters~\ref{ch:source}--\ref{ch:filling} retain this condition. The different class in Chapter~\ref{ch:quantitative-circle-trees} has its own finite-source estimates and geometric completion; it is not inserted into this recursive proof by changing the meaning of ``completed.''

The filled elementary pieces in \eqref{eq:rational-leaf} are rational tangles; see \cite{KauffmanLambropoulou2004} for their classification and fraction conventions. Our endpoint and crossing conventions are fixed above. Ordered stacking and quarter-turns of rational tangles produce Conway algebraic, or arborescent, links. Thus the construction gives a specified family within that class; it is not a classification of all arborescent knots. The choice of $\eps$ must make the final diagram one component and create no closed main component inside a box. Section~\ref{sec:pairings} gives a finite endpoint-pairing test and proves that a suitable choice exists for every allowed construction. Changing $p_\ell$ adds full twists and therefore does not change this component test.

\section{The long-twist theorem}
\begin{theorem}[Long-twist volume limit]\label{thm:main}
Fix a finite expression $C(FG)$ as in Definition~\ref{def:grammar} and a half-twist pattern $\eps$ giving a knot. There is a finite number $P(F,G,\eps)$ such that, for every independently chosen tuple of signed integers $\mathbf p=(p_\ell)$ with $|p_\ell|>P(F,G,\eps)$, the resulting knot $K_{F,G,\eps}(\mathbf p)$ is hyperbolic and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main-volume}
 \lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{2\pi}{N}
 \log\left|\langle K_{F,G,\eps}(\mathbf p)\rangle_N\right|
 =\Vol\bigl(S^3\setminus K_{F,G,\eps}(\mathbf p)\bigr).
\end{equation}
The level tends to infinity through all integers, including even and composite levels. The expression, signs and twists are fixed before that limit. The threshold depends on the fixed expression and sign pattern, but not on a comparison between the magnitudes or signs of the individual sufficiently long twists.
\end{theorem}

In concrete terms, one first chooses the entire finite arrangement of boxes and the parity of the crossings in each elementary piece. The theorem then supplies one threshold for that arrangement. Each filling integer may independently be positive or negative and exceed that threshold in absolute value. No ratio between different filling integers is bounded. Once these integers have been chosen, the conclusion concerns the single resulting knot and the sequence of its invariants as $N$ grows.

The reduced invariant $\langle\cdot\rangle_N$ has unknot value one. Chapter~\ref{ch:source} also compares it with the full modified one-cut normalization: that normalization adds the single factor $\ii^{N+3}/N$ and a unit framing character, so the exponential limit is unchanged. This factor belongs only to the marked main component. The auxiliary circles are evaluated by ordinary traces.

The isolated closures $C(h^s)$, $s\ge3$, form a separate inherited family; Section~\ref{sec:isolated} works out $C(h^3)$ explicitly. The isolated $C(hh)$ is excluded from this proof: its pure complete-color source vanishes at even levels, and its odd-level support lies on a flat boundary. This is a statement about that source, not about the invariant of a filled knot. General closures with a single elementary piece on one side, such as $C(hE)$, are also outside Theorem~\ref{thm:main}. For instance, $C(h\Turn(h^s))$ places the inner complete leaf parameters at $1/4$, outside the interval used in the first proof. Such a circle-tree presentation can instead fall under Theorem~\ref{thm:circle-quantitative} when its explicit degree and twist condition holds. The exclusions in this paragraph concern Theorem~\ref{thm:main}.

\section{An explicit criterion for circle trees}
The second construction uses the same actual stacking, quarter-turn and closing operations. At each vertex of a finite ordered tree with at least one edge put one crossing box with signed power $n_v$; its augmented version retains that box's crossing circle. Each tree edge is a quarter-turn of a child tangle. There are no unmarked product junctions, and terminal crossing circles are vertices of degree one. The cyclic order and the position of the crossing box among its children remain part of the diagram. Section~\ref{sec:circle-the-knot-construction} gives the full recursion.

If $d_v$ is the degree of vertex $v$, Theorem~\ref{thm:circle-quantitative} proves hyperbolicity and the same volume limit under the explicit condition
\[
                       \sum_v\frac{d_v}{d_v^2+n_v^2}\le2^{-25},
\]
provided the original endpoint pairing gives a knot. The angle feasibility required by the proof is automatic for this literal tree construction. Every $|n_v|\ge8192\sqrt E$ suffices when the tree has $E$ edges, but individual powers may be short or zero at vertices of sufficiently large degree. Adding an identity braid at an unmarked junction also gives an application when the sum bound holds; its added crossing circle contributes $1/d_v$, rather than zero.

The quantitative theorem uses a direct cut of the actual augmented exterior into two octahedra per tree edge. Its positive geometric path keeps track of the original primitive slopes even when their accumulated logarithms are not small. The finite invariant has a uniform main-meridian profile, with its exact scalar retained; the first correction is the preferred cusp shape of the resulting knot complement. The size of the tree and every crossing power remain fixed as $N$ grows.

Theorem~\ref{thm:local-circle-volume} gives another sufficient condition for this same literal class:
\[
                       \max_v\frac{d_v}{d_v^2+n_v^2}\le2^{-120}.
\]
Its proof evaluates each subtree as a polynomial in its remaining boundary variable and normalizes that polynomial by its exact value at zero. The next trace spends only the change of action at its current vertex. This gives a numerical bound independent of the number of vertices, although error constants and starting levels may depend on the fixed diagram. The separate geometric condition $\sqrt{d_v^2+n_v^2}\ge630$ constructs the positive metric without either quantum condition; it alone does not assert a volume limit.

Chapter~\ref{ch:directed-circle-messages} strengthens the local argument.
Theorem~\ref{thm:directed-degree-twist} proves the volume identity if
$d_v\ge2^{28}$ or $|n_v|\ge2^{27}$ at each vertex, with the alternative
chosen independently at each vertex. Its full criterion uses a separate
decay constant for each outgoing subtree polynomial and places no
outgoing requirement on the vertex evaluated last. This also constructs
knots with arbitrary fixed short-twist cores by adding even stacks of
long terminal pieces. The additions preserve the knot-component test
but generally change the knot.

\section{The preferred longitude and the classical recurrence}
For the covered strict Conway constructions, Chapter~\ref{gc:chapter} identifies
more than the volume and cusp shape. It constructs the deformation of
the original knot representation as the main meridian eigenvalue $M$
varies, and proves an explicit formula for the preferred longitude
$L$. The original order of the tangles and the changes of puncture
paths under each half twist are retained in this construction.

The proof separates two issues. The boundary pairing determines the
longitude up to a function of $M$. An algebraic argument then shows
that this function is rational, and regular representations over
every nonzero value of $M$ exclude all zeros and poles. The remaining
monomial is fixed by the preferred framing; a Seifert surface fixes
the determinant-one sign at the complete cusp.

Appendix~\ref{app:filtered-characteristic} uses the full asymptotic
expansion of the actual invariant to constrain a quantum recurrence.
Here an \emph{all-level annihilator} is a fixed nonzero operator
\[
 P(q,M,T)=\sum_{j=0}^r a_j(q,M)T^j,\qquad
 a_j\in\CC(q,M),\qquad (TF)(M)=F(qM),
\]
which annihilates the actual zero-framed reduced state at every
sufficiently large level $q=q_N$. After clearing denominators and
removing the common order of vanishing at $q=1$, its first nonzero
specialization is the \emph{classical symbol}, with the shift $T$
replaced by a commuting longitude variable. The theorem assumes such
an annihilator; it does not construct one.

Combining the source expansion with the preferred-longitude identity
shows that this symbol is divisible by the irreducible factor of the
selected geometric meridian--longitude curve. The selected curve is
the component containing the oriented discrete-faithful knot
representation, with the preferred longitude lift fixed in
Chapter~\ref{gc:chapter}. Appendix~\ref{app:geometric-characteristic}
gives the circle-tree representation and algebraic proofs.
Appendix~\ref{app:strict-tree-geometric-characteristic} treats unmarked
product junctions and transfers the intrinsic result through the
stated net-twist and closure reductions.
Chapter~\ref{ch:directed-all-orders} and
Appendix~\ref{app:directed-all-orders} supply the required expansion
for every directed certificate. The result identifies one factor,
not the entire A-polynomial or recurrence ideal, and it does not
extend the volume limits to arbitrary short twists.

\section{A guide to the proof and its terminology}\label{sec:proof-language}
The long-twist proof first studies the augmented diagram, in which the auxiliary circles are still present, and then performs their fillings by an exact finite identity. A \emph{color} is the integer label specifying the representation assigned to an auxiliary circle. At level $N$ these labels range from $1$ to $N$. Before filling, the evaluation therefore depends on a tuple of colors. We call this finite, color-dependent evaluation the \emph{source}. The \emph{complete-color source} means that every auxiliary color equals $N$; the term is distinct from completeness of a hyperbolic metric.

The algebra used to calculate a source has repeated roots. At a double root, both a polynomial's value and its derivative matter. We refer to the derivative data as the \emph{first normal data}, in the elementary algebraic sense of the coefficient of $z-c$ modulo $(z-c)^2$. Keeping these data is essential at both parities of $N$.

Expanding each elementary piece in a polynomial basis gives a finite sum. An admissible assignment of integer indices to that sum is a \emph{state}. An internal \emph{wire} is an index shared by two factors; it is not an additional physical strand. A \emph{product gate} is the coefficient for one stacking operation with two input indices and one output index. After scaling indices by $N$, these coefficients have exponential growth described by a real function, the \emph{action}. A state is \emph{strict} when all its nonforced angle inequalities are strict. The maximizer of the complete-color action determines explicit ideal hyperbolic cells. The cells have quadrilateral faces along which they are glued. The order of the four vertices specifies the face map; Chapter~\ref{ch:geometry} explains how these auxiliary gluing faces reconstruct the actual closed link diagram.

For colors other than $N$, the exact elementary evaluation splits into a principal part and a signed complementary part. We call them the \emph{core} and the \emph{alias}; the identities defining them appear in \eqref{eq:core-alias}--\eqref{eq:low-alias}. Chapter~\ref{ch:alias} proves that every term containing an alias is exponentially smaller than the leading complete action, even after arbitrary allowed composition. This is where the finite rational certificates enter the proof.

Near the remaining strict maximum, the finite sum has a holomorphic asymptotic of the form $N^{3/2}a(\chi)e^{N\Phi(\chi)}$, with $a$ nonzero. We call this local analytic contribution the \emph{packet}. The word includes a specified finite sum and a controlled error; it does not mean a formal stationary-phase expression. The complex color parameters $\chi$ are also the parameters of a marked deformation of the augmented hyperbolic structure. Identifying their peripheral holonomies fixes the absolute volume, not just its differential.

Finally, the filling identity sums the source over all colors with oscillatory coefficients. We analyze the whole sum, including the boundary integrals created when contours move and every integer Fourier mode. These modes are called \emph{charges}. The leading charges add without cancellation, and their critical action equals the volume after filling. This last step is necessary because that volume is smaller than the complete augmented volume; an estimate obtained by taking absolute values of the filling coefficients would not recover it.

\section{Background and the arguments used here}
The variational geometry belongs to the Casson--Rivin approach.
Rivin's volume methods appear in \cite{Rivin1994}; the precise
critical-point criterion for positive angle structures on an orientable
ideal triangulation with torus boundary is stated in
\cite[Theorem~1.2]{FuterGueritaud2010}. In this paper the actual cells,
face maps, peripheral curves, and end completion are constructed.
Concavity alone is not used to identify a manifold or to prove
noncancellation in a signed quantum sum.

The peripheral volume potential and its logarithmic conventions have
their standard setting in \cite{NeumannZagier1985}. Extended logarithmic
regulator data are developed in \cite{Neumann2004}. Our comparison
retains the primitive curves and fixes the additive constant at the
complete structure. The metric arguments in the appendices identify
the resulting volume with that of the original filled complement.

The classical peripheral eigenvalue curve is defined in
\cite{CooperEtAl1994}. Recurrence existence is a distinct question:
multisum elimination has the general background
\cite{WilfZeilberger1992}, colored-Jones q-holonomicity is treated in
\cite{GaroufalidisLe2005}, and the ADO family in
\cite{BrownEtAl2020}. The latter comparison keeps a framing and
variable substitution. The recurrence statements here retain the
specified state, initial data, and singular-step derivatives. The
selected-factor conclusion follows from the full source expansion
and its geometric longitude; no equality of complete recurrence
ideals is imported from these references.

Local Gaussian and stationary-phase estimates have standard analytic
background \cite{Hormander2003}. The arguments below additionally
specify the original finite lattice and measure, deform the actual
contours with their boundary terms, and control every Fourier charge.
These are the source-specific steps needed to turn a local critical
point into the asymptotic of the original invariant. The references
provide context and precise antecedents; the displayed estimates and
geometric identifications used in the theorems are proved in the
chapters and appendices indicated above.

\section{Conventions and verification}
All logarithms on a positive real interval are real. Complex logarithms and square roots continue those values on the explicitly chosen neighborhoods. We use $\arccot X\in(0,\pi)$ for real $X$ and $\ii^2=-1$. Constants may depend on the fixed construction, its selected strict stationary point and a fixed analytic neighborhood, but not on $N$. A polynomial factor means a bound by $N^C$ with such a fixed exponent.

The appendices give the finite identities and detailed analytic estimates for the long-twist argument. Chapter~\ref{ch:quantitative-circle-trees} states the summed quantitative theorem; Appendices~\ref{app:quantitative-sums} and~\ref{app:quantitative-geometry} give its complete finite-sum, ordered-cut and geometric completion proofs. The local and directed extensions are proved in Appendices~\ref{app:local-messages} and~\ref{app:directed-circle-messages}. The certificate files contain rational candidate points and exact domain subdivisions; the verifier proves their supporting inequalities using outward rational bounds. Numerical optimization is not part of verification. Chapter~\ref{ch:reproduction} explains which claims are analytic proofs, which depend on these finite certificates, and which are merely checked by optional experiments.
