\chapter{The volume theorems and their scope}\label{ch:introduction}

\section{The comparison and the order of the limits}

For a knot $K$, write $K_N(K)=\langle K\rangle_N$ for its reduced
Kashaev invariant at $q_N=e^{\pi\ii/N}$, normalized to be one on the
unknot. If $K$ is hyperbolic, the volume conjecture asks whether
\[
 \lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{2\pi}{N}\log|K_N(K)|
       =\Vol(S^3\setminus K).
\]
The right side is the volume of the complete knot complement.
Kashaev's constructions and conjecture are described in
\cite{Kashaev1994,Kashaev1995,Kashaev}; the relation with the
root-of-unity colored Jones polynomial is given in
\cite{MurakamiMurakami2001}.

This book proves the comparison under several sufficient hypotheses
on a fixed diagram. It does not claim it for every knot, every
arborescent presentation, or every short filling. The first proof is
the strict Conway theorem, Theorem~\ref{ca:main}. Its current source
is shared with the standalone Conway paper in
Chapter~\ref{ch:conway-current}. The proof expands the complete crossing powers in the pure circle
basis before estimating the resulting finite sum.

In every quantum limit below, the finite diagram, its strand
connections, disk framings, retained half twists and integer powers
are fixed first. The level then tends to infinity through all
sufficiently large integers, including both parities and composite
levels. Only auxiliary crossing-circle cusps are filled. The main
knot cusp remains complete.

\section{What a strict Conway construction records}
\label{sec:geometric-construction}

A two-string tangle has four ordered endpoints in a box. A product
stacks two boxes and joins the adjacent endpoints without changing
their order. A quarter-turn moves the entire box, including its
crossing disks. Cylindrical closure joins the exposed bottom-left
endpoint to the top-left endpoint and similarly on the right.
Chapter~\ref{ch:conway-current} fixes these connections explicitly.

An elementary augmented box consists of two strands and a circle
bounding a disk pierced by them. The Conway leaf is its clockwise
quarter-turn, with the disk carried along; it is the turned circle
$h$ defined in Chapter~\ref{ch:conway-current}. Each written leaf has
its own circle and disk framing. After choosing its retained half twist
$\epsilon_\ell\in\{0,\pm1\}$, filling the primitive slope
$\mu_\ell+p_\ell\lambda_\ell$ restores crossing power
\[
                   n_\ell=\epsilon_\ell-2p_\ell.
\]
Here $\mu_\ell$ is the circle's meridian and $\lambda_\ell$ its
disk longitude. The sign and framing conventions are part of the
diagram.

The strictness test is a finite system of real inequalities. Leaf
labels lie in $(0,1/2)$; a turn complements a label; a product with
inputs $x,y$ and output $z$ satisfies
\[
                  \max(x,y)<z<x+y<1.
\]
The final closing outputs sum to one, and their forced last product
is omitted. A construction is strict when this system has a
solution. An elementary closing child is allowed. No condition
that every leaf lie strictly above one quarter is imposed.
Theorem~\ref{ca:main} applies to any fixed strict construction
with at least two elementary pieces.

The remaining component test is topological, rather than an
inequality on the labels. The strand pairings must form one knot,
including any component that might close inside an intermediate
box. Its outcome depends on the retained crossing parities.
Adding full twists preserves it. The example and endpoint
conventions in Chapter~\ref{ch:conway-current} make both tests
explicit.

\section{Long twists and density one}

For a fixed strict construction and an admissible half-twist
pattern, the quantifiers in Theorem~\ref{ca:main} are
\[
 \exists P_0\quad
 \forall\boldsymbol p\in\ZZ^b,\qquad
 \min_\ell|p_\ell|>P_0
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 \lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{2\pi}{N}\log|K_N(K_{\boldsymbol p})|
       =\Vol(S^3\setminus K_{\boldsymbol p}).
\]
Every tuple $\boldsymbol p$ is fixed inside its limit.
There is no common-sign condition and no bound on the ratios
of the large filling integers. The threshold may depend on the
whole fixed construction and retained pattern.

This covers a set of natural density one in the assignment lattice
$\ZZ^b$. In a box $[-R,R]^b$, with integer $R$, at most
\[
 b(2\lfloor P_0\rfloor+1)(2R+1)^{b-1}
\]
tuples have some coordinate of absolute value at most $P_0$.
Their proportion tends to zero. These excluded slabs still contain
infinitely many tuples when $b\ge2$. Different tuples may represent
the same knot, so this is not a density statement about distinct
knots ordered by crossing number.

The other constructions retain their own quantifiers. For example,
an ordered circle tree with vertex degrees $d_v$ and signed powers
$n_v$ satisfies a sufficient summed test
\[
                  \sum_v\frac{d_v}{d_v^2+n_v^2}\le2^{-25}.
\]
The directed test instead permits, at each vertex, either
$d_v\ge2^{28}$ or $|n_v|\ge2^{27}$.
A high-degree vertex can therefore retain a short or zero power.
The graph and all powers are still fixed before $N$ grows.
These are sufficient tests for their stated tree construction,
not consequences of the strict Conway threshold.

\section{Why the proof separates algebra, analysis and geometry}
\label{sec:proof-language}

The \emph{finite source} is the evaluation of an augmented diagram
as a function of its auxiliary colors. The \emph{pure source}
uses no retained half twists at those circles. Its core has positive
Newton coefficients on a known support. The full physical source
also includes complementary-color terms, called aliases.
An alias is part of the finite invariant, not an approximation
error.

The proof first solves turn and closing constraints exactly.
The resulting integer lattice has cell volume one. Exact
factorial interpolation then provides the holomorphic function
needed to move contours; knowing only its values to leading
exponential order would not suffice. A strict maximum gives a
nonzero local Gaussian contribution. Separate source bounds
control the physical remainder, and a global Fourier estimate
controls the rest of the spectral cube. The whole filling-sum
theorem is what rules out cancellation by the remaining charges.

Finally, the marked geometric theorem identifies the same action,
with its additive constant and primitive disk curves, with the
volume of the specified filled complement. Equality of formal
gluing equations alone would not establish the metric or its
topology. Chapter~\ref{ch:exact-concepts} explains the exact linear
algebra used in these steps: Gram forms, Fourier orthogonality
and stationary Schur complements. Chapter~\ref{ch:reproduction}
proves the elementary final passage from a complex asymptotic
with a strict entire-remainder gap to a volume limit.

The planar constructions illustrate why the distinctions matter.
Terminal decoration can create a covered knot from a fixed
matched-sphere core, but generally changes its knot type.
For an unchanged diagram, weak balance or a complete shared-weight
certificate supplies the additional source estimate. The
geometric-contribution theorem alone leaves that estimate unpaid.

\section{How to read and reproduce the proofs}

For the first proof, read Chapter~\ref{ch:exact-concepts} and then
Chapter~\ref{ch:conway-current}. Its source is the same as the
standalone Conway paper; its imports name the finite algebra,
strict optimizer, full-source bound, whole filling theorem and
marked metric comparison separately. The canonical factorial
lemma is also available independently to the later constructions.

For the quantitative families, begin with the theorem whose test
fits the diagram, then follow its indicated finite-sum and geometric
appendices. For a planar construction, decide first whether the
diagram is decorated, weakly balanced, or certified for a gluing
operation. This determines which complementary-source estimate
is available.

Chapter~\ref{ch:reproduction} distinguishes written proofs, exact
rational certificates, optional finite checks and typesetting.
A consistency check of a dependency map does not prove the
mathematical statements at its vertices, and a finite sample of
levels does not prove an all-level asymptotic.
